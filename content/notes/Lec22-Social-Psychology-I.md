# Lec 22 — Social Psychology I

> One sentence: most of what looks like "who a person is" is actually "what situation a person is in" — the same ordinary people, under different arrangements of authority, group pressure, and diffused responsibility, will act as prison guards, executioners, or bystanders, and the mind's own explanatory machinery is built to hide this by defaulting to character.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: when someone acts a given way, how much of the explanation is who they are (disposition) and how much is where they were standing (situation) — and why do people, including trained observers, systematically get this ratio wrong in the same direction?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection insists we act from stable character — "I helped because I'm a kind person," "he yelled because he's an angry person." The situationist claim — that ordinary, psychologically healthy people will torture, betray their own judgment, or watch someone suffer without helping, given the right arrangement of social forces — feels like an excuse-making move, not a scientific one. It only becomes credible once the same manipulation produces the same behavior in dozens of ordinary strangers who share no personality trait in common. (The lecture opens with a cross-primate-species chart of neocortex size against social group size — background for *why* the human mind evolved such elaborate machinery for reading and explaining others' behavior in the first place.)
- Gap from the previous lecture: [[Lec21-Psychopathology-II]] closed the clinical unit by asking what goes wrong inside individual minds. Social psychology asks the opposite question — how far can behavior be pushed around without anything going wrong inside the person at all? It is also the direct social-level continuation of the split-brain "interpreter" from [[Lec03-Brain-I]]: there, a hemisphere confidently narrated a cause for behavior it did not generate; here, ordinary perceivers confidently narrate a personality trait for behavior a situational force actually generated. Same failure mode, scaled up from one brain to a whole society of observers — this is the loop [[Lec03-Brain-I]] explicitly forward-referenced.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition (own words) | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Causal attribution（因果归因） | Inferring *why* a behavior happened, for oneself or another person | Not the behavior itself — it's the (often wrong) story layered on top of it |
| Dispositional vs. situational attribution（内因/外因归因） | Crediting behavior to stable character/traits vs. to the external forces of the moment | Not mutually exclusive in reality; the error is treating them as if only one could be true |
| Fundamental attribution error, FAE（基本归因错误） | The systematic over-weighting of disposition and under-weighting of situation when explaining *other people's* behavior (Lee Ross) | Not the same as the self-serving actor–observer asymmetry (own behavior gets the situational excuse) — the two compound each other, see [[Lec23-Social-Psychology-II]] |
| Conformity（从众） | Changing behavior or belief because of implicit or explicit group pressure | Compliance changes behavior to avoid conflict without necessarily changing belief; obedience is compliance specifically toward a more powerful authority |
| Diffusion of responsibility（责任分散） | Each bystander assumes someone else in the group will act, so the group as a whole is less likely to act than any one person alone would be | Not simple apathy/callousness — it disappears when a bystander is alone, which callousness would not predict; compounded by pluralistic ignorance（多元无知）, misreading others' inaction as evidence nothing is wrong |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Fundamental attribution error: two designs that isolate it (Quiz-Show study; Castro study)
- **Design**: (1) Ross, Amabile, & Steinmetz (1977) — pairs of students drew cards to become "quizmaster" or "contestant"; the quizmaster wrote his own hard trivia questions (e.g., "who were the two inventors of calculus?"), the contestant had to answer them, and both the contestant and independent student observers then rated how generally knowledgeable each person was. (2) Jones & Harris (1967) — participants read a pro-Castro or anti-Castro essay and were told the writer had either freely **chosen** that position or been **assigned** it by a 50/50 coin flip, then rated how pro-Castro the writer actually was.
- **Result**: (1) The quiz format guarantees the quizmaster looks smart — contestants scored only 40% correct against questions handpicked for their obscurity. Yet observers still rated the quizmaster's general knowledge far above the contestant's (≈82 vs. ≈47 on a 0–100 scale), and even the contestants themselves rated the quizmaster higher (≈67 vs. ≈42) despite having just lived through the rigged setup. (2) Ratings of the writer's true attitude tracked the essay strongly when it was **chosen** (≈57 for pro-Castro vs. ≈22 for anti-Castro essays) — expected. But even in the **assigned**, no-choice condition, ratings still tracked the essay's content nearly as much (≈41 for pro-Castro-assigned vs. ≈22 for anti-Castro-assigned) — a truly coin-flip-respecting rater should have rated both assigned conditions the same, near neutral.
- **Inference**: both designs manufacture a situation whose power is stipulated to the rater in advance — the game format is rigged, the essay position is a coin flip — so a rational, situation-respecting observer should attribute *nothing* about character in the quiz case and rate the assigned-essay writer as attitudinally neutral. Instead people import a trait ("he's smart," "she's pro-Castro") anyway. This rules out the explanation "FAE happens because people lack information about the situation" — the information was handed to participants explicitly; the failure is a failure to **use** situational information already supplied, not a failure to have it.
- **Weakness**: contrived lab tasks (trivia contests, essay-writing) that a subject can identify as a psychology experiment; cross-cultural work (see §9) suggests the size of the correction failure is not constant across cultures.
- **Real-world stakes**: the 2004 Abu Ghraib prosecution of Pfc. Lynndie England ran this exact opposition in a courtroom. The prosecution offered a dispositional narrative ("she knew what she was doing… she is enjoying, she is participating, all for her own sick humor") engineered to produce high anger/low sympathy and a punitive sentence. The defense offered a situational narrative (she was "trying to please her soldier boyfriend," Cpl. Charles Graner, "labeled the abuse ringleader… she was a follower") engineered to produce low anger/high sympathy and a rehabilitative outcome. England was convicted on one conspiracy count, four counts of maltreating detainees, and one count of committing an indecent act, and acquitted on a second conspiracy count — the jury split the difference rather than adopting either attribution outright. The point of showing this in lecture: attribution theory is not an academic curiosity, it is literally the argument structure of a criminal trial.

### Automatic dispositional inference vs. controlled situational correction (Gilbert, Pelham, & Krull, 1988)
- **Design**: participants watched 7 silent video clips of a woman talking with a stranger; she looked visibly anxious in 5 of the clips. Participants were told she was discussing either anxiety-provoking or relaxation-inducing topics (manipulating whether a *situational* explanation for her anxiety was available), and were assigned to either just watch and judge her trait anxiety (**one task**, "unregulated") or watch, judge, **and** know they'd have to recall the topics afterward (**two tasks**, "regulated" — i.e., cognitively busy). Design: 2 (topic) × 2 (task load); hypothesis was that the one-task condition would take situational information into account and the two-task condition would not.
- **Result**: consistent with the hypothesis, participants under low cognitive load showed a large gap in trait-anxiety ratings between the anxiety-topic and relaxing-topic conditions (they corrected — anxiety displayed while discussing an easy topic was attributed to character, anxiety displayed on a hard topic was excused). Participants made cognitively busy by the recall task showed a much smaller gap — they rated her as anxious at similar levels regardless of topic, i.e., the correction step largely didn't happen. (The exact axis values on the source chart were not legible from the extracted slide; only this qualitative pattern is asserted.)
- **Inference**: this is the mechanistic version of the FAE — dispositional attribution is drawn **automatically and effortlessly** from behavior; folding in situational information to correct that first impression is a **separate, resource-dependent, controlled** step. Cognitive load doesn't change what people see, it selectively knocks out the correction stage, leaving the raw dispositional inference standing uncorrected. This rules out "people who commit FAE simply don't know the situational information" — in this design they knew it, and only failed to use it once their attention was loaded elsewhere.
- **Weakness**: a single lab operationalization of "busy" (recall a list) stands in for the many different ways attention is divided in real life; effect sizes here are described only qualitatively for the reason above.

### Conformity: the Asch line-judgment studies (with Sherif's autokinetic effect as precursor)
- **Design**: Sherif (1935) seated participants in a dark room with a single stationary point of light that appears to drift because of the **autokinetic effect（自动视动效应）**, a perceptual illusion with no real external motion; participants judged how far it moved, alone and then in groups. Asch (1951) built the sharper version: a subject sat with 6 confederates and judged aloud which of three comparison lines matched a reference line — an easy, unambiguous perceptual task. Confederates answered first and, on 12 of 18 trials, unanimously gave an obviously wrong answer before the real subject answered.
- **Result**: in Sherif's genuinely ambiguous task, individual light-movement estimates measurably converged toward a shared group norm over repeated trials. In Asch's unambiguous task, participants alone erred on less than 1% of trials, but a unanimous wrong-answering group pushed the error rate to 37%; only 25% of participants stayed independent on every single trial. Conformity rose steeply with group size from 1 to 3 confederates (3.6% → ≈32% errors) and then plateaued around 30–37% up to at least 15 confederates (Asch, 1955); a single dissenting confederate (breaking unanimity) cut conformity roughly two-thirds, from an average of about 37% down to about 10%.
- **Inference**: Sherif's ambiguous case shows people using others as a legitimate source of information under genuine uncertainty (**informational influence**) — not itself evidence against disposition. Asch's case is the sharper cut: the task has a plain, visually obvious correct answer, so a purely dispositional model ("confident, independent-minded people just say the true answer") predicts near-zero conformity regardless of audience. The jump to 37% wrong answers — and the halving-or-more effect of a single ally — shows the group's mere presence and unanimity, not the participant's private judgment or character, is driving the answer (**normative influence**, the need to be liked, compounding informational influence, the need to be right).
- **Weakness**: 1950s American undergraduate samples; a cross-cultural meta-analysis (Bond & Smith, 1996) found conformity rates vary substantially by culture (higher in more collectivist societies) and have declined somewhat in the US since the 1950s — the 37% figure is a period- and culture-bound estimate, not a fixed constant of human nature.
- **Related demonstration (compliance, not conformity)**: Freedman & Fraser's (1962) foot-in-the-door studies found fewer than 20% of homeowners agreed cold to display a large, unattractive "drive carefully" lawn sign, but over 50% agreed after first accepting a small, unrelated request (signing a traffic-safety petition) weeks earlier — a need-for-behavioral-consistency mechanism, still situational (prior small commitment), not dispositional (same homeowners either way).

### Obedience: Milgram's shock studies
- **Design**: Milgram (1961–63, Yale) recruited ordinary adults via newspaper ad ($4 + carfare) for a supposed "study of memory." Each subject became "teacher" to a confederate "learner" (never actually shocked) and was instructed by an experimenter in a lab coat to deliver escalating shocks — 15V steps up to 450V, labeled "Strong Shock" (150V) through "Danger: Severe Shock" (375V) to unlabeled "XXX" (435–450V) — every time the learner missed a memorized word pair. The learner's scripted protests escalated (complaints → screams → "my heart is bothering me!" → refusal to answer at 300V → total silence at 350V), and the experimenter's only tool was a fixed series of verbal prods ("please continue," "the experiment requires that you continue").
- **Result**: a panel of experts polled beforehand predicted only 1–3% (characterized as "psychopaths") would go all the way to 450V. In fact, **65%** of participants delivered the maximum shock, and obedience was effectively universal through the lower voltage range — discomfort was nearly ubiquitous (fidgeting, nervous laughter, pleading with the experimenter to check on the learner) but did not translate into refusal for most people until very late, if at all. Systematic variation of the paradigm showed obedience swinging from near 0% (no commands given at all, control) up to ≈65% (standard baseline, no difference by gender) depending on situational levers: authority's physical presence (remote-by-phone instructions cut obedience to ≈20%), the setting's institutional prestige (a run-down office rather than Yale dropped it to ≈48%), the authority figure's legitimacy (an "ordinary person" giving the same orders dropped it to ≈19%), the victim's proximity (same room ≈40%, forced physical contact with the victim ≈30%), and visible peer dissent (two confederates who refused to continue dropped obedience to ≈11%).
- **Inference**: this is the single strongest data point against a dispositional account of the "just following orders" defense being merely an excuse. A dispositional model predicts that only a rare, defective minority would fully comply; the fact that the *majority* of an unselected sample of ordinary adults complied, and that compliance could be dialed from ≈0% to ≈65% purely by rearranging authority, distance, and social proof — with the same kind of people each time — is direct evidence that the situation, not a "bad-apple" trait, is doing the causal work.
- **Weakness / ethics**: this is also the lecture's central cautionary tale about research ethics, not only about obedience — deceived, distressed participants who were not told the true procedure in advance are a major reason IRB-style human-subjects review exists today. A 2009 partial replication (Burger) with heavy safeguards (screening out high-anxiety volunteers, reminding subjects three times they could stop and still keep the money, an on-site clinical psychologist, and a mandatory stop at 150V as a proxy for full obedience, since going further was judged unethical even with consent) found 70% of modern participants still continued past 150V, versus 79% in Milgram's original sample at that same checkpoint — a real but necessarily partial replication. Historical reanalysis (Perry, 2013) also found Milgram's 24 experimental variants were less procedurally uniform than the published papers imply, with experimenters sometimes improvising beyond the four standard prods — a reason to treat the exact 65% figure as an estimate from one specific configuration, not a universal constant.

### Obedience and role, taken further: the Stanford Prison Experiment
- **Design**: Zimbardo (1971) recruited 24 psychologically healthy male volunteers, randomly assigned to "guard" or "prisoner" roles in a simulated prison in a Stanford building basement, planned to run two weeks.
- **Result**: guards escalated to humiliation and physical punishment — including at night, when they apparently believed researchers were not observing — while prisoners showed acute stress responses (breakdowns, a rebellion, passivity); the study was terminated after only 6 days. Two months later, one released "prisoner" (#416, held in solitary confinement for hours) described the experience in identity terms: "I began to feel that I was losing my identity… I was really my number." The lecture places Lynndie England's Abu Ghraib photographs directly alongside Stanford Prison Experiment guard photographs as a visual argument that the same situational logic — arbitrary power over a captive population, diffused oversight, a permissive institutional frame — produced comparable abuses six decades apart, in a lab and in a war.
- **Inference**: random assignment is the load-bearing design feature — nothing about who ended up a "guard" predicted sadism beforehand, so the guards' cruelty cannot be a pre-existing dispositional trait sorted into that group by the coin flip. What changed was the *role* and its permissions, which argues institutional situations can produce the behavior directly, without needing "a few bad apples" already present.
- **Weakness**: this study is now one of the most heavily disputed in the field — give ethics its due here alongside the finding. It was terminated (rightly) on ethical grounds; participants suffered real, unconsented-to psychological harm. Separately, and more damaging to its scientific status, historical work (Le Texier, 2018/2019, drawing on Zimbardo's own archived tapes) found guards were actively coached toward cruelty by Zimbardo's staff, that at least one guard later said he was "acting" based on what he thought the researchers wanted (a textbook **demand characteristic**), and that the "spontaneous" escalation was considerably more directed than the popular account suggests. It has never been formally replicated as originally run. The defensible conclusion today is closer to "a vivid, ethically catastrophic demonstration with a compromised paradigm" than "a rigorous experiment proving situational power" — the same substantive claim (situations can produce cruelty in ordinary people) is much better supported by the more carefully controlled, systematically varied Milgram paradigm above.

### Bystander effect: diffusion of responsibility across four paradigms
- **Design**: motivated by the reported 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese, allegedly witnessed by dozens of neighbors who did nothing (a detail later journalism found substantially exaggerated — see §9). Latané & Darley then ran controlled variants: (1) a fake "group discussion" over an intercom in which a confederate appeared to have a seizure, with the real participant believing 1, 2, or 5 other people were also listening in; (2) a waiting room in which a "woman" could be heard falling and moaning behind a curtain, with the subject alone, with a passive confederate, or with two genuinely naive subjects; (3) a smoke-filled-room paradigm where smoke visibly entered through a vent while the subject filled out surveys, alone, in a group of 3 naive subjects, or with 2 confederates who noticed the smoke and deliberately ignored it; (4) the "Good Samaritan" study (Darley & Batson, 1973), in which Princeton seminary students — primed by having just prepared a talk on career paths **or** on the parable of the Good Samaritan, and told they were ahead of, on, or behind schedule — encountered a slumped, groaning man in a doorway en route.
- **Result**: (1) helping during the "seizure" fell monotonically with the believed number of bystanders — 85% helped when only 1 other listener was believed present, 62% with 2 others, 31% with 5 others. (2) for the falling woman, 70% of solo subjects investigated vs. only 7% with a passive confederate present, and 40% with two naive strangers. (3) for the smoke room, 75% of solo subjects reported it within 6 minutes vs. only 10% with passive confederates present (12.5% of individuals, 38% of groups, with two other naive subjects) — yet still 75% when accompanied by a **friend** rather than a stranger. (4) for the seminarians, personality measures and even the topic of the talk they'd just prepared (Good Samaritan sermon or not) made no measurable difference to helping; being in a hurry did — 63% helped when ahead of schedule, 45% on time, only 10% when told they were late.
- **Inference**: across four independent paradigms, the single best predictor of helping was never a trait of the bystander (stated personality, religious values, even having just rehearsed the literal parable about stopping to help a stranger) — it was the number of other people present and how rushed the situation made them feel. This directly rules out the two most natural dispositional explanations for non-intervention ("callous/apathetic person," "doesn't hold the relevant values") — the same people who had just written about the Good Samaritan walked past a man in visible distress once they believed they were late. The mechanism is **pluralistic ignorance** (each bystander reads others' inaction as evidence there's no real emergency) plus **diffusion of responsibility** (each assumes someone else is already acting or better positioned to act) — both explicitly situational, group-size-driven processes, not motivational deficits.
- **Weakness**: the founding case (Kitty Genovese) itself is now known to have been substantially misreported — the "38 witnesses, no one called police" framing from the original 1964 New York Times account has been challenged by later reporting showing fewer people had a clear, unambiguous view, at least one witness shouted at the attacker, and some calls were in fact made. The effect the lecture builds on is real and independently well-replicated by Latané & Darley's controlled studies; its own origin myth is a caution about secondhand journalistic sourcing feeding into psychology curricula unchecked (see §9).

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior mainly reflects stable disposition/character | Behavior mainly reflects the situational forces acting on a person at that moment | Quiz-Show and Castro studies: raters keep crediting/blaming character even when the situation's power (rigged game, coin-flip assignment) is stipulated to them in advance |
| Atrocity requires a few dispositionally defective people ("bad apples," "psychopaths") | Ordinary, unselected people will commit or permit atrocity given the right situational architecture | Milgram: experts predicted 1–3% max compliance from psychopaths only; 65% of an ordinary sample complied fully, and the rate could be dialed by situational variables alone |
| Dispositional attribution and situational correction are one single judgment, just sometimes wrong | They are two separate processing stages — automatic dispositional read, then a distinct, effortful, resource-dependent situational correction | Cognitive busyness (Gilbert et al. 1988): loading attention doesn't change what's seen, it selectively removes the correction step, leaving the raw dispositional judgment intact |
| Bystander non-intervention reflects apathy, callousness, or urban anonymity | Bystander non-intervention is a structural byproduct of group size (diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance), reversible by making one person feel solely responsible | Seizure/smoke-room/Good-Samaritan studies: the same kind of people help far more often alone than in groups, and helping tracks headcount and time pressure, not measured personality or values |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: — not developed in this lecture; the automatic/controlled distinction (§3) is framed purely cognitively here, with no brain data presented, though it is naturally continuous with prefrontal/executive-control themes from other units.
- Cognitive/information-processing: dispositional inference as a fast, automatic, low-effort default; situational correction as a slower, controlled, resource-limited second stage that cognitive load can selectively knock out (Gilbert et al.); pluralistic ignorance as a social read of ambiguous evidence.
- Behavioral: conformity error rates, obedience compliance rates, and helping rates as a function of experimentally manipulated group size, authority proximity, and time pressure — the lecture's evidence is almost entirely behavioral/quantitative in this sense.
- Social/cultural: conformity and obedience are explicitly framed as *necessary for civilization* (coordination, social order), not intrinsically pathological; cross-cultural data show conformity is stronger in interdependent/collectivist cultures; the Holocaust and Abu Ghraib are used as the lecture's real-world stakes for why "the situation made me do it" is both scientifically supported and morally fraught.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- FAE has a built-in asymmetry via the *actor–observer* split: the "Late for an Appointment" classroom demo shows people explaining an *identical* behavior (arriving late) dispositionally when someone else does it (inconsiderate, disorganized, unmotivated) but situationally when they do it themselves (busy, traffic) — this is a demonstration with no data collected, illustrating the bias qualitatively, not a controlled study. The bias is about whose behavior is being explained, not a fixed rule about behavior in general — see [[Lec23-Social-Psychology-II]] for the data-backed version (self-serving attributional bias).
- The bias shrinks under conditions this lecture's data imply but don't test directly: explicit accountability for accuracy, low cognitive load (Gilbert et al.'s "unregulated" condition), and, per cross-cultural work, collectivist cultural frames where situational explanation is the default rather than the correction.
- Obedience and conformity are not unconditional — Milgram's own moderator data show obedience collapsing to near 0% with no authority present and to ≈11% with visible peer dissent; Asch's conformity drops by roughly two-thirds with a single ally. The "power of the situation" thesis includes the situational levers that turn it *off*, not just on.
- None of these paradigms show disposition never matters — individual variation persisted in every study (25% of Asch's subjects never conformed at all; some Milgram subjects refused early). The claim is that disposition is systematically *under*-weighted relative to situation by observers, not that it is weighted zero.

## 7. Connections
- Prior/follow-up: [[Lec03-Brain-I]] — the split-brain "interpreter" that confidently narrates a false cause for its own behavior is the individual-brain version of the attribution machinery covered here: both are fast, automatic, confident, and frequently wrong about causation; this lecture is where that forward-reference resolves. [[Lec23-Social-Psychology-II]] continues directly with self-serving attributional bias and cognitive dissonance — the *self*-directed, motivated cousin of the other-directed FAE covered here. [[Lec16-Personality]] runs the identical situation-vs-disposition argument one level down, inside personality psychology itself: Mischel's critique of trait theory (behavior is far less cross-situationally consistent than trait labels imply) is the same claim this lecture makes about social behavior specifically — two subfields converging on one conclusion about the limits of dispositionism.
- One concrete real-life example (my own): a barista who is curt with one customer and warm with the next isn't "moody" — the FAE-trained assumption — nearly as often as she's dealing with a rush, a broken machine, or the previous customer's rudeness; the Milgram/Asch data are really a formal, quantified version of "don't assume you know why," applied to a case with 65% compliance instead of a coffee order.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that situation dominates disposition, I'd cite Milgram: a panel of experts predicted only 1–3% ("psychopaths") would fully comply, but 65% of an ordinary, unselected sample did — and the rate could be moved from near 0% to 65% purely by changing the room, the authority's proximity, and peer behavior, with the same kind of people each time.
2. Rebuttal to "the Milgram/Zimbardo subjects must have just been unusually compliant or cruel people": the design itself defeats this — Milgram's subjects were an ordinary newspaper-ad sample with no personality screening for compliance, and Zimbardo's guards were *randomly assigned*, so nothing about who they were beforehand can explain the outcome; only what role and situation they were placed in can (with the caveat that SPE's internal validity is now disputed — see §9).
3. Transfer: if a workplace study found employees "become dishonest" after promotion to a supervisory role with unmonitored authority over subordinates' pay, the FAE-consistent prediction is that this reflects the situational grant of unsupervised power (Milgram/SPE logic), not that promotion selectively reveals a latent trait — the testable implication is that adding oversight/accountability (as in Milgram's remote-experimenter manipulation, which cut obedience to ≈20%) should reduce the dishonesty regardless of who holds the role.

## 9. Open Questions
- How much of the classic 65% Milgram figure and 37% Asch figure are period-bound (1950s–60s America) rather than universal? Bond & Smith's (1996) cross-cultural meta-analysis and generally lower modern Asch-style conformity rates suggest these are not fixed constants; the lecture presents the numbers without this caveat.
- The Stanford Prison Experiment's status as *evidence* (vs. a compromised, coached demonstration) is now genuinely contested in the field (Le Texier's archival work); the lecture uses it largely as if it were a clean random-assignment result. A more careful version of this lecture would foreground Milgram (better replicated, more systematically varied) and present SPE primarily as the ethics case study it undeniably is.
- The Kitty Genovese "38 witnesses" framing that motivates the whole bystander-effect section has itself been significantly revised by later journalism — worth knowing the founding anecdote is shakier than the real, well-replicated phenomenon (Latané & Darley's controlled studies) it's credited with inspiring.
- Meta-analytic work on the bystander effect (outside this lecture) finds the effect shrinks or reverses in situations that are unambiguously dangerous rather than ambiguous (e.g., Fischer et al., 2011) — the lecture's paradigms (a heard-not-seen fall, smoke, a seizure over an intercom) are all in the *ambiguous* zone; whether the same group-size effect holds for an unambiguous physical assault is a live question the lecture doesn't address.
