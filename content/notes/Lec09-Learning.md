# Lec 09 — Learning

> One sentence: learning is how an organism turns repeated experience into a prediction about the future — but *what* gets learned is not arbitrary association-by-pairing; biology (preparedness) and cognition (contingency) constrain and sometimes bypass simple reinforcement.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: how does experience — as opposed to genes — durably change future behavior, and what rules govern which associations actually get learned?
- Why it isn't obvious: naive intuition treats learning as either (a) totally arbitrary — any stimulus can be linked to any response or outcome given enough repetitions (a "blank slate" view), or (b) simple habit-stamping — whatever behavior gets rewarded just mechanically gets stronger, full stop. Both intuitions are falsified by specific, demonstrable exceptions in this lecture.
- Gap from previous lecture: [[Lec08-Consciousness]] asked what an organism is aware of at a given moment; this lecture asks how repeated experience durably reorganizes *future* behavior — the mechanism that turns momentary experience into lasting adaptation. This is also the closing lecture of the pre-Exam-1 arc (the session page marks the next session as "Exam 1"). Forward, [[Lec10-Memory-I]] and [[Lec11-Memory-II]] take up how experience is stored and retrieved once "learned" in the sense defined here.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Classical conditioning（经典条件反射） | A neutral stimulus, repeatedly paired with a stimulus that already elicits a reflex, comes to elicit that reflex itself | Confused with operant conditioning: nothing the organism *does* matters here — the UCS arrives regardless of behavior; this is a new stimulus→reflex link, not a new action |
| Operant/instrumental conditioning（操作性/工具性条件反射） | An action's own consequences (reward or punishment) change how often that action is emitted in the future | Confused with classical conditioning: there is no pre-existing reflex being transferred to a new stimulus — the organism's spontaneous behavior is what's being selected |
| Law of Effect（效果律） | Thorndike's principle: responses followed by satisfying consequences are "stamped in," responses followed by discomfort are "stamped out" | Not the same as a reinforcement schedule — it's the founding *principle*; reinforcement type/schedule are later elaborations of it |
| Preparedness（生物预备性） | Species carry evolved biases that make certain CS–UCS pairings easy to learn and others nearly impossible, independent of contiguity | Not "general learning ability" — the same rat learns a taste–illness pairing easily and a taste–shock pairing poorly; the *content* of the pairing matters, not exposure count |
| Contingency（相倚性） | What gets conditioned is not mere temporal co-occurrence (contiguity) but whether a stimulus reliably *predicts/adds information about* the outcome | Confused with contiguity: two stimuli can co-occur equally often yet condition differently if one is redundant and carries no new predictive value (blocking) |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Pavlov: classical conditioning of salivation
- **Design**: a fistula in the dog's cheek collects saliva; a neutral stimulus (tone/bell/metronome) is repeatedly paired with food (UCS) placed in the mouth.
- **Result**: after pairing, the tone alone (now CS) elicits salivation (CR) that is functionally the same response as the original unconditioned response (UCR) to food; CR strength rises steeply with early paired trials and then plateaus (a negatively-accelerated acquisition curve).
- **Inference**: an entirely new stimulus→response link can be built by nothing but temporal pairing with an existing reflex — Aristotle's "law of association by contiguity" made experimentally tractable. The bell comes to mean "food is near"; any UCS (not just food) works, which is why Gabrieli's classroom demos substitute a puff of air ("CAN") or a popping balloon and get the identical UCS/UCR/CS/CR structure.
- **Weakness**: single reflexive/autonomic response in one species; by itself doesn't distinguish whether contiguity (mere pairing) or contingency (predictive information) is doing the causal work — that question is only settled by Rescorla and Kamin below.
- **Further properties demonstrated**: extinction（消退）— the CR weakens over non-reinforced trials but can spontaneously recover after a rest period, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases the association; a generalization gradient（泛化梯度）— the CR is strongest to the trained CS and falls off for similar stimuli; discrimination training（辨别训练）— pairing a CS+ with the UCS while a similar CS− is never paired sharpens that gradient around CS+; second-order conditioning（二阶条件反射）— an already-established CS (bell) can itself function as a UCS to condition a brand-new stimulus (a black square), showing conditioning can chain beyond the original biological reinforcer.

### Rescorla & Kamin: contingency and blocking — contiguity is not enough
- **Design**: Rescorla — two groups receive the same *number* of CS–UCS pairings; in the "random" group the UCS also occurs on its own, so the CS is not a reliable predictor, while in the "contingency" group the UCS occurs only when preceded by the CS. Kamin (blocking) — Group 1 first conditions CS1 (tone) alone to a UCS (food), then receives a compound CS1+CS2 (tone+light) with the same UCS; Group 2 receives only the CS1+CS2 compound from the start, with no prior CS1 training.
- **Result**: Rescorla's contingency group conditions strongly to the CS; the random group shows little or no conditioning despite identical raw pairing counts. Kamin's Group 1 shows little or no conditioning to CS2 (light) when tested alone afterward; Group 2, without prior CS1 training, conditions normally to CS2 even though CS2 was paired with the UCS exactly as often in both groups.
- **Inference**: conditioning tracks whether a stimulus carries *new, non-redundant predictive information* about the UCS, not how often it merely co-occurred with it. Rescorla shows raw pairing frequency is insufficient; Kamin sharpens this to show that even a perfectly-paired CS2 fails to condition if CS1 already "used up" all the UCS's predictive value. Together this is the clearest evidence that classical conditioning is a computation over information, not a mechanical stamping-in from contiguity alone.
- **Weakness**: both are presented in the lecture as schematic designs without reported quantitative conditioning strength or species/sample details; the argument is about the qualitative existence of the dissociation, not effect size.

### Watson: Little Albert — classical conditioning of fear
- **Design**: an infant ("Little Albert"), who initially showed no fear of a white rat, had the rat (CS) repeatedly paired with a loud, startling noise (UCS) that reliably produced a fear/startle reaction (UCR).
- **Result**: Albert came to cry and withdraw from the rat alone (CR); the fear generalized to other furry/similar stimuli (rat, rabbit, fur) but the slide explicitly notes it did **not** transfer to a wooden block or a piece of cloth.
- **Inference**: this is a classical-conditioning demonstration, not an operant one — a reflexive UCR (fear/startle) is being transferred to a new stimulus purely by pairing, structurally identical to Pavlov's dog, just with an emotional rather than a digestive reflex. It is easy to misfile Little Albert as "operant/behaviorist" because it appears in the lecture immediately beside Watson's behaviorist manifesto (S→R, no reference to the mind), but no consequence of Albert's *behavior* is being manipulated — only a stimulus–stimulus pairing. The failure to generalize to a wooden block (an emotionally/biologically irrelevant object) is itself an early preparedness result, previewing §4's spine.
- **Weakness**: n = 1, no informed consent by modern standards, Albert was reportedly never deconditioned; the study could not be ethically replicated today.

### Thorndike: puzzle box and the Law of Effect
- **Design**: a cat is placed in a box that can only be escaped by performing a specific response (e.g., pulling a loop that releases a latch); food sits outside. Latency to escape is timed across repeated trials.
- **Result**: cats show highly variable trial-and-error behavior on early trials (scratching at bars, pushing at the ceiling, digging, howling); escape latency drops gradually and noisily across roughly 60 trials (not a sudden insight) until pulling the latch dominates the response repertoire.
- **Inference**: no UCS is being paired with anything — there is no pre-existing reflex to transfer to a new stimulus. Instead, the *consequence* of a spontaneously emitted action (escape → food) determines whether that action becomes more or less likely in the future (the Law of Effect: reward strengthens, no reward weakens, punishment weakens strongly). This is operant conditioning's founding logic, genuinely different in kind from Pavlov's classical conditioning.
- **Weakness**: small, individually variable samples; "trial and error" alone doesn't rule out some partial insight for other species — a boundary the lecture leaves open.

### Skinner: shaping and the partial-reinforcement effect
- **Design**: a novel operant response (e.g., a rat pressing a high lever it has never touched) is built by successive approximations（渐进塑造法） — reinforcing first any orientation/location near the lever, then facing it, then stretching upward, then touching it with the paws, then finally pressing it, withdrawing reinforcement from each step once the next-closer approximation appears. Separately, one group of animals is reinforced on 100% of trials and another on a partial schedule (~30%); both are then tested to extinction (reward withheld entirely).
- **Result**: shaping builds a response the animal had never spontaneously performed, purely through successive reinforcement of approximations. In the reinforcement-schedule comparison, the 100%-reinforced group's running speed collapses quickly once reward stops; the ~30%-partially-reinforced group's response persists markedly longer before extinguishing (the partial-reinforcement effect: more resistant to extinction).
- **Inference**: reinforcement doesn't just strengthen an existing behavior — it can *construct* behavior that never occurred spontaneously, answering "where does a genuinely novel operant response come from?" And reward *predictability* itself changes how conditioning is unlearned: an animal that has already learned reward is unreliable takes longer to conclude reward has stopped for good — extinction is itself an inference process, not passive decay.
- **Weakness**: shaping depends on an experimenter's judgment calls about "closer approximations," hard to standardize; the partial-reinforcement effect's direction is robust but the lecture gives no quantitative extinction-trial counts, only the qualitative running-speed curves.

### Seligman: learned helplessness in dogs
- **Design**: Phase 1 — dogs are yoked in a hammock and shocked; Group A can terminate the shock by pushing a panel near its nose, Group B's shocks are simply yoked to Group A's, so both groups receive an equal number and duration of shocks, only *controllability* differs. Phase 2 — both groups face avoidance learning in a shuttle box: a tone (CS) precedes shock, and jumping a barrier within 10 seconds avoids the shock entirely.
- **Result**: Group A (previously *could* control shock) learns to avoid readily, with response latency dropping from near the 10-second cutoff down into the 1–3 second range within about a dozen trials. Group B (previously *could not* control shock) shows a flat median latency pinned near the ceiling (~60 sec, i.e., failing to escape within the test window) across nine trials — showing motivational deficits (slow to initiate even known escape actions), emotional deficits (listless, frightened, distressed), and cognitive deficits (poor learning even in this new, genuinely controllable situation).
- **Inference**: the operative variable is not the amount of aversive stimulation (equated between groups) but the earlier experience of *no contingency* between behavior and outcome; that experience generalizes into an expectation of helplessness that persists even into a different, actually-controllable situation. The lecture extends this to human depression via explanatory style: whether a setback is attributed as internal/external, global/specific, and stable/unstable in cause predicts whether it produces pervasive helplessness or stays contained.
- **Weakness**: an acute inescapable-shock paradigm in dogs is ethically fraught by modern standards, and its extrapolation to human depression is a strong structural analogy, not a demonstrated causal mechanism in humans.

### Garcia & Koelling: taste aversion and biological preparedness
- **Design**: rats drink water carrying two separable cues at once — a taste (sweet) and an audiovisual cue (bright-noisy) — and are then either made ill (X-ray or lithium chloride, inducing nausea) or given electric shock (a non-gastric aversive UCS). The 2×2 crosses which CS (taste vs. light/noise) with which UCS (illness vs. shock).
- **Result**: a full double dissociation, quantified in fluid-intake terms. After illness, flavored-water intake drops to roughly **~20%** of normal (strong aversion) while light/noise-water intake stays at roughly **~95–100%** of normal (no aversion). After shock, light/noise-water intake drops to roughly **~20%** of normal (strong aversion) while flavored-water intake stays at roughly **~95–100%** of normal (no aversion).
- **Inference**: which CS binds to which UCS is not arbitrary. Rats are biologically prepared to link *tastes* specifically to *internal illness* (an evolutionarily sensible pairing — bad food causes sickness) and *external* cues specifically to *external* pain, regardless of how the pairings are contrived. This directly refutes the equipotentiality assumption behind classical behaviorism — that any stimulus can condition to any response with equal ease given enough pairings.
- **Weakness**: taste–illness learning is also famous for tolerating hour-long CS–UCS delays (violating standard contiguity requirements), which the lecture doesn't quantify further here; the rat findings are extrapolated, not directly shown, to humans.
- **Also noted (same preparedness logic, different species/domain)**: pictures of snakes/spiders (CS) paired with shock (UCS) condition a skin-conductance fear response (galvanic skin response / sweat, the UCR) more readily than pictures of flowers/mushrooms paired with the identical shock — no percentages given in the lecture, but the qualitative pattern parallels Little Albert's fear generalizing to furry/animal-like stimuli but not to a wooden block.

### Tolman & Honzik (1930): latent learning
- **Design**: three groups of rats run a maze once daily. Group 1 is rewarded with food at the goal every day from day 1. Group 2 is never rewarded. Group 3 is never rewarded for the first 10 days, then rewarded from day 11 onward.
- **Result**: Group 1's errors decline steadily from roughly 9–10 on day 1 to about 1.5–2 by day 17. Group 2's errors decline only modestly and plateau around 6–7. Group 3 tracks Group 2's mediocre, unrewarded performance (~7–8 errors) through day 10 — but the day after its first reward (day 12), its error rate drops sharply to roughly 2.5–3, converging with Group 1's low error rate by day 14–17.
- **Inference**: Group 3 must already have learned the maze's layout — a cognitive map（认知地图） — during the ten unrewarded days, even though that learning produced no visible improvement in performance until reward gave the animal a reason to express it. This separates *learning* (acquiring knowledge) from *performance* (using it), which a strict reinforcement-only Law of Effect cannot explain, since by that account no learning should be accruing at all on the unrewarded days.
- **Weakness**: maze-error curves are noisy and the "abrupt drop" argument rests on a single transition point (day 11→12); the lecture reports no statistical test.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviorism / general-process learning — universal, species-general laws of contiguity and reinforcement; any stimulus can link to any response given enough pairings (equipotentiality); nothing is learned without reinforcement (strict Law of Effect) | Biological-constraint / cognitive learning — learning is shaped by evolved, content-specific biases (preparedness) and by computed predictive relationships (contingency, informativeness) and cognitive representations (maps) that can form without reinforcement | **Garcia & Koelling**: taste binds to illness, not to shock, regardless of pairing — refutes equipotentiality. **Kamin's blocking**: identical contiguity, different conditioning, because CS2 is redundant — refutes pure contiguity. **Tolman & Honzik**: learning with zero reinforcement, revealed only once reward makes performance worth showing — refutes a strict reinforcement-only Law of Effect |
| Watson's "black box" behaviorism — describe only observable S→R relations, no reference to internal computation | Rescorla's cognitive reframing — even classical conditioning is computing the *predictive/information value* of a CS about a UCS | **Rescorla's contingency design**: equal pairing frequency, different conditioning strength, because only one group's CS is actually informative |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: — not developed in this lecture; no brain regions or circuits are named. A natural forward pointer to reward-circuitry material in a later Emotion/Motivation lecture.
- Cognitive/information-processing: contingency and blocking show conditioning strength tracks the *information value* of a CS about a UCS, not pairing frequency; latent learning shows a cognitive map can be built and stored with no immediate behavioral payoff; explanatory style (internal/external, global/specific, stable/unstable) is a cognitive-appraisal layer sitting on top of raw reinforcement history.
- Behavioral: acquisition curves, extinction and spontaneous recovery, generalization gradients, discrimination training, second-order conditioning, shaping via successive approximations, the partial-reinforcement effect, avoidance-learning latency curves.
- Social/cultural: learned helplessness reframed as a model of human depression via explanatory style; delayed gratification (§6) predicting decades-later social/academic outcomes; language acquisition (§6) as a domain where social reinforcement (parental correction) demonstrably fails to explain what children actually learn.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- **Contingency in preverbal infants**: a 2-month-old whose head movements are wired to move a crib mobile learns quickly to smile and coo while turning the mobile; a yoked infant receiving the identical number of mobile movements, just not contingent on its own head movement, does not learn to smile or coo at all — the same contiguity-vs-contingency lesson as Rescorla's animal work, now shown in preverbal humans.
- **Reward value is relative, not absolute (negative contrast)**: in the lecture's graph, a group whose reward is downgraded partway through training ends up running *slower* than a control group that had the poorer reward the whole time — so operant behavior tracks a comparison against an expected reward level, not the objective reward magnitude, which a simple stamping-in account cannot capture. (The slide gives the graph without narrating the exact switch point in words, so treat the specific design details as approximate.)
- **Delayed gratification** (Mischel; n = 653 children of faculty/graduate students, ages 4–5): offered one marshmallow now or two after waiting 15 minutes, only about **30%** of children waited the full 15 minutes. Wait time correlated roughly a decade later with fewer behavioral problems and better academic/social outcomes — the lecture cites an approximate **210-point SAT gap** between children who waited only ~30 seconds versus the full 15 minutes. This shows an individual-differences, self-control component to "reward" that plain conditioning theory (treating reward magnitude/schedule as the whole story) does not address.
- **When rewards harm (the overjustification effect，过度理由效应)**: a rat that runs in a wheel "for fun," once rewarded with food for running, will run *less* than its pre-reward baseline once the food reward is later removed; preschoolers who draw for fun, then rewarded with gold stars (a conditioned reinforcer), draw less once the stars stop, compared to children who were never rewarded at all. Adding an extrinsic reinforcer to an already-intrinsically-motivated behavior can *reduce* the behavior below its unreinforced baseline — the reverse of what a naive "reward only ever increases behavior" reading of the Law of Effect predicts.
- **Language acquisition resists a conditioning account** on several fronts presented in the lecture: a 4-day-old French infant already prefers French over Russian, Italian, or backwards French (prenatal exposure, prior to any postnatal reinforcement); 1-month-olds can discriminate phoneme contrasts (e.g., Czech, Hindi, Ntlakapamux/Inslekampx) that neither they nor their parents' native language even distinguishes, so parental correction cannot be shaping this; in the lecture's example a parent corrects meaning, not grammar ("Mamma isn't a boy, he's a girl" / "That's right" — ignoring the ungrammatical utterance entirely), yet children's grammar improves anyway; children produce sentences they have never heard (generativity) and make overgeneralization errors ("my teacher holded the rabbit") that were never modeled for them — together pointing to a rule-extracting mechanism that operant reinforcement of correct utterances is not doing the work of.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. If I had to prove to a non-psychologist that reward alone doesn't explain learning, I'd cite Tolman & Honzik: rats given zero reward for 10 days already knew the maze's layout, revealed the instant a reward was introduced — learning had been happening with nothing to "stamp it in."
2. Rebuttal to "of course animals just repeat whatever gets rewarded, that's obvious": the overjustification effect predicts the opposite in a specific case — adding a reward to an already-enjoyable behavior can reduce that behavior below its unrewarded baseline, which a naive "reward always increases behavior" intuition can't accommodate. Separately, Kamin's blocking shows that even within straightforward pairing-based learning, an equally-paired stimulus can fail to condition if it's redundant — so raw repetition count was never the operative variable to begin with.
3. Transfer: a call center switches agents from a flat salary to per-call pay. Prediction: call volume/speed should rise (a straightforward operant effect of a new positive reinforcer) — but for any agents who found the work intrinsically rewarding (genuinely enjoying helping customers), the overjustification effect predicts their off-metric effort (patience, off-script problem-solving) may drop once the extrinsic incentive is introduced, even as raw call counts rise.

## 9. Open Questions
- Where is preparedness actually implemented — a difference in perceptual salience, in the speed of the association-forming circuitry, or a dedicated neural pathway (is taste-aversion learning literally different circuitry from cue–shock learning)? Not addressed here; this is exactly the kind of gap a later Emotion/Motivation lecture might fill.
- How much of second-order conditioning and blocking can be captured by a single formal learning rule (e.g., a Rescorla-Wagner–style model), versus the lecture simply presenting the phenomena without committing to a mechanism? Not resolved here.
