# Lec 01 — Introduction

> One sentence: psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior, and this opening lecture makes its case experientially rather than by assertion — showing, through illusions and live demonstrations, that what we see, hear, know, remember, feel, and do is **constructed** by the mind, not a faithful recording of the world, which is exactly why a science of mind is both necessary and possible.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: what kind of thing is psychology, and can something as private and seemingly self-transparent as "the mind" be studied with the rigor of a natural science — and if so, at what level (brain? person? group?) should it be studied?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection reports a seamless, faithful window onto reality — "I just see what's there, I remember what happened, I know how I'd feel and act." If the mind already tells us the truth about itself, it seems to need no science, or to be the wrong kind of object for one (too private, not physical). The lecture's own "psychocentric view" diagram (after Peter Gray, *Observer*, Oct. 2008) puts psychology at a hub connecting natural sciences (biology, chemistry, physics), social sciences (anthropology, sociology, economics, political science), and humanities (art, music, language, philosophy, theology) — which is itself a claim under dispute: is psychology *really* one of these, borrowing methods from the others, or a distinct science of its own?
- Gap from the previous lecture: none — this is the first lecture. But its entire content (illusions, the McGurk effect, automaticity errors, misjudged geography, mispredicted feelings) is built to open a gap rather than close one: if introspection is this unreliable about our own minds, then "it seems true to me" cannot be the standard of evidence for a psychological claim. That gap — *if common sense can't be trusted, what can?* — is exactly what [[Lec02-Science-and-Research]] is built to answer.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Levels of analysis（分析层次）: brain / person / group | Kosslyn & Rosenberg's framework: mental life simultaneously involves brain events, person-level content/process (knowledge, attention, memory), and group-level relationships (culture, social interaction), each modifying and triggering the others | Not reductionism — the claim is *not* "only the brain level is real and the rest reduces to it"; all three levels are treated as real and bidirectionally interacting |
| Constructive perception（知觉建构） | What we perceive is actively assembled from sensory input plus built-in and learned assumptions, not read off the retina/eardrum like a recording | Not "your senses are broken" — the same inferential shortcuts that create illusions in artificial displays are what make normal, real-world perception fast and usually correct |
| Selective attention / inattentional blindness（选择性注意 / 非注意盲视） | Conscious awareness is gated by what you're attending to; a stimulus can strike the eyes and still go completely unreported | Not "the information never reached the senses" — it may be briefly available (cf. Sperling's sensory store, [[Lec10-Memory-I]]) but fails to reach the level attention/report requires |
| Automaticity（自动化加工）: power and peril | Heavily practiced processing (fluent reading, word recognition) runs without deliberate, top-down monitoring — fast and effortless, but it substitutes the statistically expected pattern for the literal input when the two diverge | Not carelessness or low effort — it is what *expertise* looks like; the same automaticity that lets you read at speed is what makes you blind to a duplicated word or a rearranged sign |
| Affective forecasting（情感预测） | Systematic mispredicting of the intensity and duration of one's own future feelings and behavior in an emotionally or morally charged situation | Not the same as hedonic adaptation — adaptation is the *mechanism* (feelings fade back toward baseline); forecasting failure is the *mistaken prediction* people make before that adaptation happens |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Visual illusions parade (Müller-Lyer arrows; Ebbinghaus–Titchener circles; Shepard's "Terror Subterra" figure/ground creatures; checker-shadow illusion; named-only: Criss-Cross Effect, the Koffka Ring, White's Illusion, Simultaneous Contrast, Shepard's Tables, Stepping Feet, Shading/Depth/Faces)
- **Design**: static two-dimensional displays in which physically identical elements (line lengths, circle diameters, patch lightness) are embedded in different surrounding contexts.
- **Result**: no numbers are given — these are live classroom demonstrations, not data. Reported outcome is qualitative: equal-length lines with inward vs. outward arrowheads look unequal; a red circle surrounded by large neighbors looks smaller than the identical circle surrounded by small neighbors (Ebbinghaus/Titchener); two patches of identical grey on a checkerboard-under-a-shadow look like different shades (Adelson's checker-shadow illusion — per the session page's own "Removed Clips" note, the live in-class attempt at this one reportedly failed to render correctly).
- **Inference**: perception is not a transparent readout of the proximal stimulus but a construction that factors in context (relative size of neighbors, apparent lighting and shadow) — the same visual machinery that normally computes real-world size and surface lightness correctly is fooled once a display isolates a cue (e.g., local contrast) from the real-world regularity (e.g., consistent lighting) it normally tracks.
- **Weakness**: no citation, no participant count, no measured effect size — classroom demonstrations only. They motivate the idea of construction; they do not by themselves establish *how* construction works.

### McGurk effect (McGurk, H., & MacDonald, J., *Nature*, 1976)
- **Design**: a video dub in which the auditory track plays the syllable "BA" while the on-screen speaker's lips are shaped for "GA."
- **Result**: 98% of adults report *hearing* "DA" — a syllable that is neither the audio nor the visual component actually presented.
- **Inference**: what we perceive as "the sound heard" is a fusion computed jointly from auditory and visual input, not a transcription of the sound wave; the illusion persists even once the viewer is told the trick, which shows the fusion happens automatically, upstream of belief.
- **Weakness**: a single classic, widely replicated demo, but the slide gives no sample size behind the "98%" figure beyond "most adults"; the effect's strength is known (from the wider literature, not stated on this slide) to vary by phoneme pairing, language, and individual — a minority of people do not fuse at all.

### Selective-attention counting video ("How many times do the people in the white shirts pass the basketball?", shown via YouTube; the session page's own "Removed Clips" note identifies this as the classic Daniel Simons "Selective Attention Test," in the tradition of the Simons & Chabris (1999) inattentional-blindness paradigm)
- **Design**: viewers are given a specific counting task (passes between players in white shirts) while a separate, salient, task-irrelevant event unfolds on screen at the same time.
- **Result**: not shown numerically on this slide — a live audience demo, no data reported in class. (The wider literature on this paradigm, not cited here, finds that a large fraction of viewers fail to notice the irrelevant event despite looking directly at it.)
- **Inference**: attention functions as a gate on awareness — when it is fully committed to one feature of a scene, a conspicuous, unattended event can go completely unregistered in conscious report even though it reached the retina.
- **Weakness**: no numbers reported for this specific audience; by 2011 many students may already recognize this now-famous clip, which would bias an in-class replication toward a higher notice rate than the original studies found — a threat this slide doesn't address.

### Automaticity errors (misread "ONE WAY / NOT DO ENTER" sign; unnoticed duplicated word in "PARIS IN THE THE SPRING"; the "FINISHED FILES ARE THE RE-SULT OF YEARS OF SCIENTIFIC STUDY..." F-counting task; Stroop color-word Sets #1 and #2)
- **Design**: four different tasks that each hide a small anomaly inside heavily over-learned processing — two proofreading illusions (a reordered sign, a repeated word inside a familiar phrase), a letter-counting task where target letters appear in a low-salience phonetic context ("of"), and the classic Stroop task (name the *ink color* a word is printed in, where the word itself names a conflicting color).
- **Result**: no numbers are reported on the slides for this audience — all four are live demonstrations. Each is the standard way of eliciting well-established findings: fluent readers routinely miss the duplicated "the," misread an unusual sign as the far more common "DO NOT ENTER," undercount the letter F in function words like "of" (the F there is pronounced /v/), and are measurably slower and more error-prone at naming ink color when the printed word names a different color (Stroop, 1935).
- **Inference**: over-practiced processing substitutes the statistically expected pattern for the literal input rather than checking it character-by-character — efficient (whole-word and whole-phrase recognition needs no per-element effort), but blind to local violations of the expected pattern, and it wins by default over a competing deliberate task (naming ink color) even when doing so is exactly what you were told not to let happen.
- **Weakness**: three of the four (sign, Paris triangle, F-count) are informal, uncited classroom proofreading illusions with no reported error rate for this audience; only Stroop has a large, independently quantified literature behind it (the slide itself shows no numbers, only the two word-color sets used to run the demo live).

### Geography east/west, north/south trivia (San Diego vs. Reno; Philadelphia vs. Rome; Atlanta vs. Chicago; Portland vs. Toronto; Miami vs. Santiago)
- **Design**: audience is asked to judge the relative compass position of city or country pairs chosen specifically because the true answer runs counter to the pair's category-level (state/country/continent) position.
- **Result**: no data collected or reported in class — presented as a live quiz. (Each pair is a known "surprise" case: e.g., San Diego, CA is in fact farther east than Reno, NV, even though "California is west of Nevada" is generally true; Rome is farther south than Philadelphia despite "Europe feels northern.")
- **Inference**: geographic knowledge is not stored as a mental map with accurate coordinates for every place; it is organized hierarchically by category (state, region, country), and when asked about a specific pair, the category-level relationship is retrieved and substituted for the specific fact — producing confident, systematic error even from people who know the individual cities.
- **Weakness**: the slide gives no citation and no measured error rate; the demo's persuasiveness depends entirely on the live audience's own surprise, which these notes cannot independently verify was the majority response.

### Affective forecasting: tenure/lottery/paralysis anecdotes, and Kawakami, K., et al., "Mispredicting Affective and Behavioral Responses to Racism," *Science* 323(5911): 276–8 (2009)
- **Anecdotes (uncited on the slide)**: "if I don't get tenure, I will be sad ... if I do get tenure I will be happy" → two years later, no difference between the groups; "if I win the lottery, I will be happy!" → a year or two later, no difference; an accident leading to quadriplegia or paraplegia → ratings return to typical (pre-accident) levels within about three months. No numbers, sample, or citation are given for these three examples — they are stated as established generalizations from the affective-forecasting research literature, not presented as a specific study with a design.
- **Design (Kawakami et al., the one item in this block with a full citation and data)**: "Forecaster" participants imagined a scripted scenario and predicted their own emotional distress and which of two people (a Black confederate or a White confederate) they would choose as a partner; "Experiencer" participants actually lived through the same scenario. In it, a Black male and a White male are in the room; the Black male leaves to get his phone and, in passing, gently bumps the White male's knee; the White male then says either nothing (control), a moderate slur ("Typical, I hate it when Black people do that"), or an extreme racial slur. Afterward, all participants rated their negative emotional distress and chose a partner for an anagram task.
- **Result** (read from the slide's bar charts, approximate): Forecasters predicted noticeably higher distress than Experiencers actually reported in every condition (roughly 7–7.5 vs. ~4 out of a ~9-point scale after a slur, versus ~4.5 vs. ~3 with no comment). On the behavioral measure, Forecasters said they would choose the White partner only about 10% of the time after the moderate slur and about 24% after the extreme slur — i.e., they predicted they would pointedly shun him — but Experiencers who actually went through it still chose the White partner roughly 65% and 62% of the time, respectively, barely below the ~53–68% baseline with no comment at all.
- **Inference**: people have a systematic gap between forecasted and actual affect and behavior, specifically in morally charged situations — they overestimate both how upset they would be and, more strikingly, how much their moral disapproval would actually redirect their behavior. This is a concrete demonstration of why self-report ("I would never...") is a poor predictor of what people actually do, and it is the lecture's own worked example of "how we feel vs. how we act."
- **Weakness**: Forecasters and Experiencers are different people (a between-subjects design), so part of the gap could reflect who volunteers for which role or how each group wants to appear, not a true within-person forecasting failure; a lab confederate's scripted remark is a narrow stand-in for real-world racism, and choosing a partner for a low-stakes anagram task is a narrow stand-in for real discriminatory action. The tenure/lottery/paralysis anecdotes carry none of this design detail at all and should be weighted accordingly.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Structuralism (Wundt; Titchener) — the right unit of analysis is the *elements of consciousness* (sensations, feelings), found through trained introspection | Functionalism (James, following Darwin) — the right unit is the *adaptive function* a mental process serves for the organism in its environment | No head-to-head experiment in this lecture; the reading's own critique of introspection (it cannot establish that mental images exist or are universal, and much mental processing is inaccessible to it at all) undercuts structuralism's *method* specifically — which is why functionalism (and later behaviorism) displaced it by attrition rather than by a single decisive study |
| Behaviorism (Watson, Thorndike, Hull, Skinner) — only directly observable stimulus–response history is legitimate scientific data; unobservable "mind" is out of bounds | Cognitive psychology (the "cognitive revolution," Newell, Simon, Chomsky, late 1950s–1970s) — the mind is like software running on the brain's hardware, and characterizing that software (unobservable representations and operations) is the actual object of study | This lecture's own demos are implicit evidence for the cognitive side: McGurk fusion and the automaticity/proofreading errors are not predicted by any reinforcement history — they require positing an internal representation (a fused phoneme, an expected word template) that overrides the literal stimulus. A pure stimulus–response account has no mechanism for why seeing lips shaped for "GA" changes what an unrelated "BA" soundtrack is heard as |
| Behaviorism and psychodynamic theory (Freud) together — the mind is either off-limits to study or driven by unconscious forces the person cannot access or control; little room for self-directed change | Humanistic psychology (Maslow; Rogers) — people have free will and an inborn drive toward growth, so therapy should be non-directive and supportive rather than analytic or reinforcement-based | Not an empirical dispute raised in this lecture — humanistic psychology arose explicitly as a values-driven "third force" reaction against the other two, not as a response to a discriminating study. Flag this as a disagreement about scope and values, not one this lecture's evidence adjudicates |

Later syntheses mentioned in the assigned reading but not framed as opposed to anything here: cognitive neuroscience (blends cognitive psychology and brain-level methods to show *which* brain systems implement *which* mental processes) and evolutionary psychology (Cosmides, Tooby, Buss, Pinker — treats some cognitive strategies as products of natural selection). Both are presented as later additions to, not combatants against, the schools above.

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: barely touched — one photograph of a human brain, and the assigned reading's brief mention of brain-scanning (e.g., amygdala activation as a marker of emotional reaction) as a *method* for studying the mind. The neural level is deliberately deferred to [[Lec03-Brain-I]] and [[Lec04-Brain-II]].
- Cognitive/information-processing: the dominant level of this entire lecture — every demo (illusions, McGurk, selective attention, automaticity, geographic knowledge, affective forecasting) concerns how a mental representation is built, retrieved, or predicted, and where that construction goes wrong.
- Behavioral: the actually measured output in each block — a verbal report of "DA," a pass count, a misread sign, a quiz answer, a chosen anagram partner.
- Social/cultural: the "psychocentric view" diagram explicitly frames psychology as bridging natural sciences, social sciences, and humanities; the Kawakami study is explicitly social (racism, workplace slurs — the slide notes that 33% of white respondents report having heard anti-Black slurs in their own workplace), previewing the social-psychology material much later in the course.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- Almost everything in this lecture is a **demonstration, not an experiment**: no sample sizes, no control conditions, and (with the single exception of Kawakami et al.) no statistics are reported. A show of hands or a gasp in the lecture hall is not evidence that an effect is population-general or robust — that is precisely the gap [[Lec02-Science-and-Research]] exists to close.
- The near-universality of the illusions (almost everyone in the room sees them) is itself informative — it suggests the underlying inferential process is a shared property of normal perceptual systems rather than something learned idiosyncratically — but this lecture offers no manipulation (e.g., comparing observers from different visual environments) that would actually test that claim.
- The affective-forecasting anecdotes (tenure, lottery, paralysis) are stated with no citation, sample, or number at all; they should be treated as claims-to-be-verified, in sharp contrast to the Kawakami study a few slides later, which comes with a design, a citation, and readable data.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that we can't trust our own predictions about how we'd feel or act, I would cite Kawakami et al. (2009): it isolates people who only *imagine* a racist incident from people who actually *live through* one, and shows both a distress gap and a much larger behavioral gap (predicted ~10–24% chance of still picking the White partner vs. an actual ~62–65%) — real data, not an anecdote about tenure or lottery winnings.
2. Rebuttal to "these are just cute party tricks, not science": Stroop and McGurk are not one-off anecdotes — they are precisely quantifiable and reliably reproduced in any population tested (color-naming reaction time reliably slows under interference; ~98% of adults report the same fused syllable). A phenomenon that fools nearly everyone, in the same direction, on demand, is exactly the kind of law-like regularity a science can be built around — that's a stronger, not weaker, claim than "common sense."
3. Transfer: if I'm deciding whether to move to a new city, the mechanism here predicts an "impact bias" — I will overestimate both how happy the good parts will make me and how unhappy the hard parts will make me, and underestimate how much I'll adapt back toward my baseline within a few months, the same pattern shown by the tenure/lottery/paralysis anecdotes (though those specific claims were not sourced on this slide).
