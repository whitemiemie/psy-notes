# Lec 08 — Consciousness (Blindsight & Neglect)

> One sentence: the brain routinely **processes** information — location, motion, coarse shape, even emotional valence — that never becomes something the person can **report**, so "I saw/felt nothing" is a claim about awareness, not proof that the information wasn't registered.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: is subjective awareness ("what it's like to see/know something") the same thing as the brain having processed that information, or can the two come apart?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection is our only normal window onto our own processing, so the naive intuition is that "I processed X" and "I am aware of X" are the same fact described twice. There is no everyday experience of *unconscious* seeing to make the alternative intuitive.
- Gap from the previous lecture: [[Lec07-Attention]] established that attention selects a small slice of the sensory world for full processing. This lecture asks the sharper question hiding inside that one: what happens, behaviorally and neurally, to the information that attention *doesn't* select, or that damage removes from the "aware" pathway entirely — does it vanish, or does it keep influencing behavior from below awareness? Blindsight (cortical/geniculostriate damage) and neglect (parietal/attentional damage) are the two clinical routes into that question.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Blindsight（盲视） | After V1/geniculostriate damage, above-chance visual discrimination (location, motion, coarse shape) in the "blind" field despite the patient denying any visual experience there | Not malingering or lucky guessing — accuracy is far above chance (e.g. 97% on long/short line); also not full vision — fine shape discrimination (square/rectangle) fails |
| Tectopulvinar / geniculostriate systems（顶盖-丘脑枕系统 / 膝状体纹状系统） | Two of three subcortical retinal projections: geniculostriate (LGN→V1, ~90% of fibers, supports conscious vision) vs. tectopulvinar (superior colliculus→pulvinar, subcortical, supports blindsight) | Not "one pathway is vision and the other is blindness" — both carry visual information; only one normally supports *reportable* vision |
| Neglect（单侧忽视） | Failure to report, respond to, or orient toward stimuli on the side opposite a (usually right-hemisphere) lesion, not explained by elementary sensory or motor loss | Not the same as hemianopia (a visual-field cut) — a hemianopic patient without neglect compensates by moving the eyes; a neglect patient with an intact field still misses that side |
| Anosognosia（疾病感缺失） | Denial of, or failure to recognize, one's own deficit (e.g., insisting a paralyzed arm works) | Not confusion or dementia — it is selective (patient reasons normally about everything else) and, per Ramachandran, reversible for ~90 min by caloric (cold-water ear) stimulation |
| Disengage deficit（注意脱离缺陷，Posner） | The specific attentional failure in parietal neglect: patients can *engage* attention on the neglected side and can *move* attention there, but cannot *disengage* attention once it is captured on the intact side | Not a general attention deficit — engage and move are near-normal; only the disengage step, and only when the invalid cue sits ipsilesionally, is impaired |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Blindsight: patient DB, patient TN, monkeys, and hemispherectomized infants
- **Design**: Weiskrantz's patient DB had an arteriovenous malformation destroying right occipital (visual) cortex, producing a left visual field (LVF) scotoma. Tested by dynamic perimetry and forced-choice guessing (presence/absence of light, line orientation, long-vs-short line, shape pairs) in the "blind" field, despite DB reporting no visual experience there. Parallel evidence: patient TN (bilateral occipital strokes 36 days apart, completely cortically blind — de Gelder's case); monkeys with left striate cortex + callosal splenium ablations (Cowey & Stoerig, 1995) tested on touch-the-light-location vs. report-blank tasks; two infants with hemispherectomy in the first year of life (Braddick et al., 1992) tested with single vs. competing peripheral stimuli.
- **Result**: DB moved his eyes toward, and reached more accurately for, lights he denied seeing; better than chance on presence/absence and line orientation; **97% accurate** discriminating long vs. short line at exposures as brief as 100 msec; **~90% accurate** on circle/cross and X/O; but could **not** do square/rectangle discrimination. In an extinction-style task, a distractor placed in the blind field slowed responses to a target in the *intact* field (500 vs. 359 msec) — so blind-field information still competed for attention even though never reported. TN reportedly navigated a cluttered obstacle-filled hallway without collisions despite total cortical blindness. Monkeys: in the simple "touch the light" task, blind animals performed as well as the intact animal in both fields; but in the version requiring a "blank" response, probe trials in the blind field were **treated as blank** — the animals' own behavioral report system, not just their perception, showed the blind/aware split. Infants: isolated targets in the "blind" visual field elicited fixations at rates comparable to the intact field early on, but by 10–12 weeks post-surgery, **competing** stimuli in the intact field suppressed blind-field fixations to near zero — described in the lecture as the "growth of a conscious visual system" that comes to dominate the older route.
- **Inference**: near-normal forced-choice performance coexisting with flat denial of any visual experience is a direct demonstration that visual information can be processed — localized, guided toward, discriminated in coarse shape and orientation — without entering consciousness. This licenses the pathway story: the geniculostriate system (90% of retinal fibers, cortical, V1-dependent) supports *conscious, reportable* vision; the tectopulvinar system (superior colliculus → pulvinar, subcortical) supports the *residual, unreportable* capacities that survive V1 loss. That the effect replicates across an unrelated human case (TN), a nonverbal species (monkeys, where "report" is itself a trained button-press rather than language), and a developmental case (infants, where the "conscious" route visibly comes online and begins to override the older one) rules out the objection that blindsight is a language/demand-characteristics artifact of asking a human patient leading questions.
- **Weakness**: DB is n=1 with years of repeated testing (practice and subtle cueing cannot be fully excluded); the failed square/rectangle discrimination shows blindsight is crude, not full vision minus awareness — only some visual computations (location, motion, coarse orientation/shape) survive; TN's obstacle course is an anecdote, not a controlled trial; the monkey/infant data show *processing* survives but, lacking verbal report, cannot directly confirm *what is or isn't consciously experienced* — the inference from behavior to "unconscious" always carries this gap.

### Anosognosia and confabulation (Ramachandran)
- **Design**: bedside interview and demonstration tasks with patients denying left-arm paralysis after right-hemisphere stroke — asked to point/clap with the paralyzed hand, offered money to perform two-handed tasks (screw in a lightbulb, $5; tie shoelaces, $10), handed a tray of full cocktail glasses to hold. Then: ice-cold water irrigated into the left ear canal (vestibular caloric stimulation), followed by the same self-report questions.
- **Result**: patient insists she is pointing/clapping/holding the tray with both hands while the paralyzed arm visibly does not move and the tray tumbles; confronted with failure, she rationalizes ("it's my arthritis"). After caloric stimulation her eyes begin to move (nystagmus) and, asked "can you use your hands?", she now answers accurately: "I can use my right arm, but not my left arm. I want to move it, but it doesn't move," and reports the paralysis has lasted "several days" — but the insight fades and she returns to denial roughly **90 minutes** later.
- **Inference**: the fact that a transient physiological manipulation (cold water in one ear) can turn off anosognosia for an hour and a half, without touching the arm itself, proves that "the arm is paralyzed" and "the patient does not know the arm is paralyzed" are two separable facts produced by different brain states — anosognosia is not a symptom of general confusion (the patient reasons fluently about everything else, including inventing a rationalization) but a specific, reversible failure of self-monitoring/belief-updating.
- **Weakness**: single dramatic case demonstrations with no reported sample size, effect size, or replication in the slides; the mechanism linking vestibular stimulation to hemispheric arousal/self-monitoring is not explained; this is a striking demonstration, not a treatment (the effect is minutes-to-an-hour, not lasting).

### Measuring and localizing neglect
- **Design**: convergent behavioral batteries — copying/drawing (clock faces, house-and-tree scenes), cancellation (mark all the A's on a cluttered page), line bisection (mark the midpoint of a horizontal line, with letters placed at one or both ends, or with the marking hand's start position varied), reading (patient V.S.N., who read only the words to the right of a vertical bar bisecting each line — Kartsounis & Warrington, 1989), and visual/tactile "scan path" exploration of a room or tabletop (Karnath, *Nature*, 2001).
- **Result**: clock and scene copies compress or omit left-sided elements (all 12 hours crammed into the right half of the dial; left trees/windows missing from a house). A patient with a *visual field cut but no neglect* catches every target on a cancellation task by moving his eyes; a patient *with neglect but no visual field cut* misses every left-sided target — the double dissociation. Line-bisection bias (marking far right of true center) is **improved** by placing a single letter at the neglected left end for the patient to read first, **worsened** when letters appear at both ends, **greatest** when the letter is at the right end only, and shifts with the hand's starting position — showing the bias is graded and manipulable, not fixed. Visual and tactile exploration both show the same rightward-shifted "center" of search in neglect patients versus a symmetric center in controls. Anatomically, the lecture contrasts the classical view (lesion = parietal cortex, where the deficit is measured) with Karnath's newer account: lesions in **superior temporal cortex** produce ipsilateral hypofunction and contralateral hyperfunction in the (structurally intact) parietal cortices, which rebalance as the patient recovers.
- **Inference**: the convergence of writing, drawing, cancellation, bisection, and touch on the *same* rightward-biased center argues neglect is a unitary spatial-attentional bias, not a bundle of unrelated task deficits; the hemianopia/neglect double dissociation rules out "neglect is just blindness." Karnath's imaging reframes neglect the way [[Lec03-Brain-I]] warned lesion studies must be read in general: the region where the *deficit shows up* (parietal) need not be the region that is *damaged* (temporal) — a network-imbalance story, not a simple map-the-lesion-to-the-function story.
- **Weakness**: the graded, context-sensitive line-bisection results mean no single test reliably estimates the "true" severity of neglect in a patient; the temporal-vs-parietal lesion debate is presented as an unresolved "old idea / new idea" contrast without quantitative lesion-overlap data in these slides.

### Posner's engage/disengage/move paradigm — localizing the mechanism
- **Design**: cued reaction-time task. A cue (central arrow or peripheral box) indicates a location with 80% validity (20% invalid, plus a neutral condition); subjects press a button on detecting a target "X" in either box. Normal subjects (Posner et al., 1980) and parietal-stroke patients were compared.
- **Result**: normal subjects show the standard gradient — fastest RT to validly cued targets (~240 msec), intermediate for neutral (~260 msec), slowest for invalidly cued targets (~300 msec). Parietal-stroke patients show only a **mild** deficit when validly cued to the contralesional (neglected) field (so they *can* engage attention there) and a roughly normal cost when attention must *move* there from a neutral start — but a **dramatically enlarged** cost (RTs around 900–1100 msec vs. 500–600 msec elsewhere) specifically when an invalid cue has parked attention in the intact, ipsilesional field and the target then appears contralesionally.
- **Inference**: decomposing attention into engage / move / disengage localizes the deficit to disengagement alone — patients are not blind to the neglected side and not unable to move attention there; they are specifically unable to let go of attention once it is captured ipsilesionally. This is the mechanistic center of gravity for the whole neglect literature in this lecture: it rules out both a pure-sensory account (engagement contralesionally is nearly normal) and a pure-motor/output account (movement of attention there is nearly normal).
- **Weakness**: based on small clinical samples and RT as an indirect window onto an unobservable internal state ("attention"); disengagement failure alone does not explain the anosognosia data above or the representational (imagined/object-based) results below, so it cannot be the whole story of neglect.

### Neglect follows the mental map and the object, not just the retina
- **Design**: (a) Bisiach & Luzzati (1978): Milanese neglect patients, life-long-familiar with the Piazza del Duomo, asked to describe the square from memory while imagining standing at one end facing the cathedral, then from the reverse viewpoint. Bisiach, Luzzati & Perani (1979): patients viewed random cloud-shaped stimuli through a narrow slot (so the whole shape was never visible at once) and judged whether two sequentially presented shapes were the same or different. (b) Behrmann & Tipper (in Umiltà & Moscovitch, 1994): a two-circle "barbell" figure shown either static or physically rotating 180°, with targets appearing on either circle.
- **Result**: (a) from imagined viewpoint A, patients reported only the landmarks that would fall on their right from that vantage point; from the opposite imagined viewpoint B, they now reported the buildings they had *omitted* the first time — the same physical piazza, two different halves reported, purely as a function of imagined heading. In the cloud/slot task, patients made more same/different errors specifically when the shapes differed on the (left) neglected side — despite the shape never being visible as a retinal whole. (b) In the static condition, patients were slower to detect targets in the LVF than RVF as expected; once the barbell physically rotated, patients became slower for RVF targets and faster for LVF targets — the "neglected side" **moved with the object** as it rotated.
- **Inference**: both results show neglect operates over an internal, updateable representation of space — an imagined scene never fully seen at once, or an object-centered reference frame that travels with a rotating shape — not merely a fixed retinotopic half-field. This is the conceptual bridge from blindsight (a subcortical sensory-routing story) to neglect (a higher-level representational/attentional story) that structures the lecture as a whole.
- **Weakness**: rotation effects are reported from a handful of patients (five, in the RT/error charts shown); the imagined-piazza task depends on each patient's pre-existing, unstandardized spatial memory of a real place; neither result cleanly distinguishes an "object-centered" frame from a viewer-centered frame that simply re-anchors with attention.

### Neglected information still shapes behavior without reaching awareness
- **Design**: a forced-choice preference paradigm — two versions of a picture (e.g., a house) identical except that one shows flames on the left (neglected) side; patients first give an explicit same/different judgment, then a forced preference ("which would you rather live in").
- **Result**: explicit report shows **no difference** detected between the two images (consistent with left-sided neglect), but on forced choice patients reliably **avoid** the burning house.
- **Inference**: this is the neglect-side counterpart of blindsight — information from the neglected field is processed to a level sufficient to bias a preference/choice even though it never becomes something the patient can report seeing. Together with blindsight, it is the lecture's central argument that "processed" and "consciously reportable" are dissociable properties of information in the brain, not two names for the same fact.
- **Weakness**: presented as a single demonstration with no sample size or replication data in these slides; a forced preference above chance is also compatible with a degraded/dim conscious percept the patient can't articulate, rather than fully zero conscious content — the slides include no confidence-rating or metacognitive measure that would rule this out, so the "unconscious" label should be read as the lecture's working interpretation, not an airtight proof.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Neglect is a sensory deficit (like a field cut) | Neglect is an attentional/representational deficit, dissociable from sensation | A hemianopic-but-not-neglect patient compensates by eye movements and scores normally on cancellation; a neglect-but-not-hemianopic patient still misses left targets. Posner's disengage-specific RT cost, and the imagined-piazza/rotating-barbell results (deficit follows a mental map or an object, not the retina), only make sense on Position B |
| Neglect's cortical locus is parietal (classical view — lesion sits where the deficit is measured) | Neglect arises from superior-temporal-cortex lesions producing secondary parietal imbalance (Karnath) | Imaging showing the lesion focus in temporal cortex with ipsilateral parietal hypofunction / contralateral parietal hyperfunction, rebalancing with recovery — a pattern the classical "parietal = the deficit's home" view does not predict |
| Neglected/blindsight information is not processed at all (a true, total sensory gap) | It is processed to a level that can guide discrimination, action, or preference, just not reportable awareness | DB's 97%/90% above-chance discrimination; monkeys' and infants' preserved localization/fixation; the burning-house forced-preference result in neglect — none of these are explicable if the neglected/blind-field information were simply absent |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: geniculostriate (LGN → V1, ~90% of retinal fibers) vs. tectopulvinar (superior colliculus → pulvinar) visual pathways; occipital pole / V1 damage (DB, TN); parietal cortex (areas ~39/40) as the classical neglect locus; superior temporal cortex (areas ~22/42) as Karnath's proposed primary lesion site; right-hemisphere dominance for both neglect and anosognosia (~5% of right-sided lesions produce anosognosia).
- Cognitive/information-processing: dissociation between processing and reportable awareness; Posner's engage / disengage / move decomposition of spatial attention; viewer-centered vs. imagined/object-centered spatial representations; explicit verbal report vs. implicit forced-choice/preference as two different assays of "what the brain knows."
- Behavioral: above-chance guessing on shape/orientation/line-length in blindsight; RT costs/benefits in cued attention tasks; omissions in drawing, cancellation, and reading; rightward bias in line bisection, tunable by cueing and hand-start manipulations; confabulated verbal justifications under direct bedside questioning.
- Social/cultural: anosognosia has real clinical stakes — a patient who believes she can walk or use both arms will resist rehabilitation and safety precautions "because nothing is wrong," which is a social/caregiving problem, not just a lab curiosity; the lecture does not develop this thread beyond the bedside vignettes.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- Blindsight is crude, not full vision minus the light of awareness: DB succeeded at long/short line and circle/cross/X-O discrimination but **failed** at square/rectangle — only a restricted set of visual computations (coarse localization, motion, gross shape/orientation) survive on the subcortical route.
- Neglect ≠ blindness: a clean visual-field test does not clear a patient of neglect, and an intact visual field does not rule it out either — both must be tested and interpreted separately (the hemianopic-vs-neglect double dissociation on cancellation tasks).
- The caloric "cure" for anosognosia is transient (~90 minutes) and mechanistically unexplained here — a striking demonstration that awareness of a deficit is a separable, manipulable brain state, not evidence of a lasting treatment.
- The unconscious-preference (burning house) result in neglect is a single demonstration in these slides, not a large replicated literature the way DB's blindsight discriminations are; treat it as suggestive rather than as firmly established as the blindsight case, and note it could reflect a degraded/dim conscious percept rather than zero awareness — the slides provide no confidence-rating data to decide this.
- Object-based/rotating-barbell neglect is reported from a small clinical sample (five patients in the RT charts shown) — the same small-n caution that applies to split-brain findings in [[Lec03-Brain-I]].

## 7. Connections
- Prior / follow-up: [[Lec03-Brain-I]] already used split-brain callosotomy patients to show a hemisphere can act on information the speaking hemisphere denies knowing about, and a confabulating "interpreter" generating fluent, false explanations — this lecture's anosognosia (fluent rationalization: "it's my arthritis") and the unconscious burning-house preference are the *same logical move* (report ≠ processing) applied to right-parietal/right-hemisphere lesion patients rather than callosotomy patients; the split-brain evidence itself is not repeated here. [[Lec07-Attention]] is the direct predecessor (what gets selected for full processing); [[Lec15-Emotion-and-Motivation]] revisits the report/no-report gap in a different register (subjective feeling vs. measurable physiological arousal), and the general theme of unawareness of one's own internal state resurfaces with real health consequences in [[Lec19-Stress]].
- One concrete real-life example (my own): swerving a bicycle to avoid a pedestrian who stepped off a curb on my visual periphery, only realizing afterward that I never consciously "saw" them step out — the swerve shows my visual system registered position and trajectory well enough to generate a correct motor response before or without full conscious report, a small, healthy-brain analogue of the blindsight/neglect processing-without-report gap.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that "seeing" and "knowing you're seeing" are different things, I would cite DB's 97% accuracy discriminating long vs. short lines flashed for 100 msec in his blind field while he denies any visual experience there — the number itself, far above the 50% chance floor, is what "he's just guessing" cannot explain.
2. Rebuttal to "the neglect patient must just not be trying, or is confused": the Posner disengage-specific RT pattern refutes this directly — the same patient shows a nearly normal cost engaging attention on the neglected side and a nearly normal cost moving attention there from neutral, with the huge cost appearing *only* in one specific transition (disengaging from an invalid ipsilesional cue). A general confusion or motivation account would not predict a deficit that surgically selective.
3. Transfer: showing a right-parietal neglect patient a rotating pinwheel where a target starts in their intact field and is carried by rotation into their neglected field, the object-based-neglect result (Behrmann & Tipper) predicts the patient will be slow to detect the target once it enters the neglected side *of the object*, even at an instant when it is still physically located in their good hemispace.

## 9. Open Questions
- What does the superior-temporal lesion actually do to intact parietal cortex to produce the hypo-/hyperfunction pattern (Karnath) — disconnection, an inhibitory imbalance, or competitive attentional dynamics? Not mechanistically specified in these slides.
- Why is anosognosia specifically a right-hemisphere phenomenon in only ~5% of relevant cases, and why does caloric stimulation reverse it for roughly 90 minutes and no longer — what does the time course imply about what normally suppresses the false "I'm fine" belief?
- Is "disengage" a single neural operation, or could the Posner engage/disengage/move triad hide several independently failable subprocesses?
- How much of blindsight and unconscious neglect-preference reflects a genuinely zero-awareness process versus a dim, hard-to-articulate conscious percept — the slides include no metacognitive/confidence measure that would adjudicate this, which is the reason to treat the "unconscious" framing here as the lecture's working interpretation rather than a settled fact.
