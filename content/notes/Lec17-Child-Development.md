# Lec 17 — Child Development

> One sentence: infants arrive with far more machinery than they can *show* through action, so cognitive development looks less like Piaget's four neat qualitative stages and more like a continuous unmasking of early competence as the motor/executive systems needed to act on it come online.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: what do infants actually know about the world, and does that knowledge unfold through discrete, universal, qualitative **stages（阶段）** (Piaget) or continuously?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection gives no access to one's own infancy, and infants cannot report on their own minds. The two available intuitions both fail: a strict **nativism（先天论）** ("some knowledge is innate" — Plato, Descartes, Kant) predicts too much too early, while a strict **empiricism/blank-slate** view (Watson: "give me a dozen healthy infants... I'll guarantee to train any one at random...") predicts too little too late. The actual data show a mixed pattern — some capacities (imitation, small-number sense) present within days or months, others (explicit false-belief reasoning) waiting years.
- Gap from previous lecture: the course's nature/nurture and localization themes ([[Lec03-Brain-I]]'s question of whether a function is built-in versus learned) here get an ontogenetic version — Piaget's entire theoretical project, per the slides, was explicitly to **reconcile** nativism and empiricism rather than pick a side.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Object permanence（客体永久性） | Understanding that an object continues to exist once out of sight/reach | Not the same as *reaching correctly* for a hidden object — looking and reaching can dissociate in the same infant at the same moment |
| A-not-B error（A-非-B 错误） | Infant repeatedly searches at the location where a toy was *previously* found (A) rather than where it was *just* hidden in view (B) | Easy to over-read as "no permanence concept at all"; the lecture's own neuroimaging data suggest it may instead be a prefrontal/inhibitory-control limit riding on top of an earlier-emerging concept |
| Assimilation vs. Accommodation（同化 vs. 顺应） | Fitting new input into an existing mental structure vs. revising the structure itself | Both together = Piaget's "adaptation"; assimilation alone would predict no learning ever changes the child's categories |
| Habituation / violation-of-expectation looking time（习惯化/违反预期注视法） | Infant is habituated to a stimulus, then shown a possible or "impossible" outcome; longer looking at the impossible outcome is read as registering the violation | Not a direct readout of "belief" — it is an inference from looking time, and what exactly drives the extra looking (novelty vs. violated physical expectation) is contested |
| Theory of mind（心理理论） | Attributing to another person a mental state (belief, desire) that can diverge from reality | Not the same as simple perspective-taking (seeing that someone has a different *viewpoint* on the same reality) — the diagnostic test is representing a **false** belief |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Imitation in neonates
- **Design**: adult experimenters sat face-to-face with infants only days old and modeled tongue protrusion, mouth opening, or lip pursing.
- **Result**: infants reliably reproduced the modeled gesture within days of birth.
- **Inference**: matching an observed facial gesture to one's own (unseen) face requires some cross-modal mapping between vision and one's own motor system that an infant has had essentially no time to learn from feedback — evidence against a strict blank-slate account, for at least this capacity.
- **Weakness**: the slide gives no sample size or statistics; "matching" was presumably coded by observers who could see which gesture was modeled, an obvious blinding concern the lecture does not address.

### Object permanence: Piaget's search task and its frontal-lobe correlate
- **Design**: hide a toy repeatedly under blanket A while the infant watches and retrieves it; then, still in view, move the toy to blanket B and let the infant search. Separately, Baird et al. used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to measure frontal-lobe blood flow while infants either succeeded or failed to reach for a hidden object.
- **Result**: infants under ~8 months do not search for a hidden object at all ("out of sight, out of existence"); infants who do search (from ~8 months) commit the **A-not-B error**, reaching to A even though they just watched the toy hidden at B. The NIRS data show a clear rise in frontal-lobe blood flow specifically when the child successfully reaches for the hidden object ("object permanence achieved"), and a flat/negative signal when the child fails to reach.
- **Inference**: the timing of the reaching-based transition tracks frontal-lobe (executive/motor-planning) maturation — which licenses reading the *manual-search* version of the object-permanence task as partly an index of prefrontal readiness, not purely of when the underlying concept is acquired.
- **Weakness**: correlational infant neuroimaging (motion artifact, small samples typical of infant NIRS); by itself this does not show the concept existed earlier — that argument needs the looking-time evidence below.

### Baillargeon's "Magical Events" and the looking-vs-reaching dissociation
- **Design**: Baillargeon's preferential-looking paradigm habituates infants to an event, then shows either a physically possible or a physically "impossible" (magical) outcome, measuring looking time (long looking = perceived novelty/violation). Separately, a well-known demonstration seats a 7-month-old in front of two covered wells; the infant watches a toy placed in well B, both wells are covered, and the infant is filmed *looking* at well B while it *reaches* to well A (the location that had previously paid off).
- **Result**: infants look reliably longer at physically impossible outcomes, months before they can pass the manual object-permanence test. In the two-well footage, the same infant, at the same moment, gazes at the correct well (B) while its hand uncovers the wrong one (A).
- **Inference**: sustained looking at a violated physical expectation — and a hand and an eye disagreeing within one trial — together show that the infant's perceptual/attentional representation of the object's location can be more accurate than its motor response. Piaget's manual-search criterion therefore **underestimates** how early the underlying knowledge is present; the "stage boundary" partly reflects the response system, not the concept.
- **Weakness**: looking time is an indirect proxy — longer looking could reflect low-level perceptual novelty rather than a violated conceptual expectation about permanence specifically; this exact ambiguity is why the "Criticism of Piaget" slide flags looking-time-based ages as revising, not just confirming, Piaget's stage-like picture.

### Theory of mind: from goal-detection to explicit false belief
- **Design**: (1) Woodward (1998) habituated 6- and 9-month-olds to a hand reaching for one of two objects along a fixed path, then tested whether infants looked longer when the *object* reached for changed (same physical motion, new goal) or when the *path* changed (same goal, new motion); 3-month-olds were tested the same way. (2) Wimmer & Perner's (1983) Sally-Anne task: Sally hides a ball in a basket and leaves; Anne moves it to a box; the child is asked where Sally will look for it. Wellman, Cross, & Watson (2001) meta-analyzed many replications of this design.
- **Result**: 6- and 9-month-olds looked longer when the *goal object* changed, tracking intention rather than literal motion; 3-month-olds instead looked longer at the new *motion path*. On the Sally-Anne task, 3-year-olds answer "the box" (predicting from the true, updated location), while 5-year-olds answer "the basket" (correctly predicting from Sally's outdated, false belief).
- **Inference**: by 6 months, infants already parse an observed action in terms of its goal rather than its trajectory — a precursor to representing others' mental states that predates verbal false-belief understanding by years. The 3-to-5-year shift on the verbal task marks a further, distinct achievement: representing that someone can believe something that is no longer true, not merely see things from a different vantage point. The consistency of the 3-vs-5 split across many replicated studies (not a single lab's quirk) is what upgrades this into a real developmental claim.
- **Weakness**: goal-attribution from looking time cannot fully rule out a lower-level "habituate to the object, not the goal" account. The Sally-Anne task also carries verbal and inhibitory-control demands (the child must suppress the salient true location) — exactly the looking-vs-acting confound seen in object permanence, meaning the age boundary is at least partly method-dependent rather than purely conceptual.

### Autism and a selective theory-of-mind deficit
- **Design**: compare typical 4-year-olds against IQ-matched 6-12-year-olds with autism on (a) the standard false-belief task and (b) a "false picture" control with matched memory/inhibition demands but no need to represent another mind (a photo shows an object in a location; the object is then moved — is the photo still accurate?).
- **Result**: typical 4-year-olds outperform the autistic group on false belief (~65% vs. ~23% passing) but the pattern reverses on the false-picture control, where the autistic group does somewhat better (~84% vs. ~53%).
- **Inference**: this double dissociation shows the autism-associated deficit is specific to reasoning about **mental** representations, not a general deficit in reasoning about any outdated representation — evidence for a genuine, selective theory-of-mind impairment rather than blanket cognitive delay.
- **Weakness**: a single comparison without full sample details on this slide; autism is clinically heterogeneous (prevalence ~1/140, male:female ratio 4:1–10:1 per the slide), and treating "theory-of-mind deficit" as a complete account of autism is not something this lecture attempts to defend further.

### Early number sense
- **Design**: infant addition/subtraction — one or two dolls are placed behind a screen, one is added or removed out of view, and the screen drops to reveal either the arithmetically correct or an "impossible" number of dolls, with looking time as the measure. Separately, 6-month-olds were shown two picture panels (2 items vs. 3 items) while a loudspeaker played synchronized drumbeats of 2 or 3 ("boom, boom" vs. "boom, boom, boom").
- **Result**: infants look longer at arithmetically impossible outcomes; on the cross-modal task, infants reliably looked at whichever panel's object count matched the number of drumbeats they heard.
- **Inference**: a small-number sense that **generalizes across sensory modality** (sound to sight) is present by 6 months, before any number words — support for an innate, non-linguistic "number sense" restricted to very small sets.
- **Weakness**: only tested for 2-vs-3-type comparisons; says nothing about whether *exact*, larger-number arithmetic is innate.

### Counting across cultures: where nativism stops
- **Design**: members of the Pirahã tribe (a culture/language reported to lack number words beyond very small quantities) were asked to match or reproduce sets of 1–10 objects.
- **Result**: near-100% accuracy for 1–2 items, dropping sharply after 3, and near floor for 8–10 items.
- **Inference**: this cleanly splits the nativism/empiricism question by content rather than forcing an either/or answer — "one, two, many" may be innate, but *differentiating* larger exact quantities depends on a culturally transmitted counting system (words/numerals), directly bounding how far the innate number-sense claim above can travel.
- **Weakness**: a single population case study with no sample size or procedural detail given; unfamiliarity with the specific test format is a standard confound in "innateness" claims from cross-cultural number cognition and is not addressed here.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Piaget: fixed, universal, qualitative stages — competence is genuinely *absent* until the stage-appropriate structure develops (e.g., no object permanence before ~8 months) | Continuous/early-competence account — the underlying concept is present much earlier; classic tasks under-detect it by conflating conceptual knowledge with motor/executive performance | The NIRS frontal-lobe timing data support a real maturational component in the *reaching* version of the task, while Baillargeon's looking-time paradigm and the well-A/well-B looking-vs-reaching dissociation show knowledge present months earlier — and the lecture's own "Criticism of Piaget" slide states children pass many Piagetian milestones, including object permanence, earlier than his norms |
| Nativism/rationalism — some knowledge (imitation capacity, small-number sense) is innate | Empiricism — the mind starts as a blank slate, filled in by experience (Watson) | Neonatal imitation and the 6-month cross-modal number-matching study argue for innate content; but the Pirahã data show differentiating *large* numbers is not innate but culturally/linguistically scaffolded — the lecture's own resolution is "both, split by content and set size," not a clean either/or |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: fNIRS shows frontal-lobe blood flow rising specifically when an infant succeeds (versus fails) at manual object-permanence search — the only neural data point in this lecture, and it targets executive/motor-planning maturation rather than the concept itself.
- Cognitive/information-processing: assimilation vs. accommodation as the two-part adaptation mechanism; goal-based (not purely motion-based) parsing of others' actions from 6 months; false-belief representation as a genuine meta-representational achievement; a small-number sense that generalizes across sound and vision.
- Behavioral: neonatal imitation; the A-not-B search error; the looking-vs-reaching dissociation; failure of conservation on liquid/mass/number tasks (a preschooler insists the taller, thinner glass "has more," or that a rolled-out clay "hot dog" now has more clay — all classroom demonstrations of the same underlying limitation, **centration/perceptual dominance**, with no separate data given per demo); autism's selective pattern on false-belief versus false-picture tasks.
- Social/cultural: the Pirahã case shows a culture's number-word system bounding what individual counting cognition can do; autism's diagnostic triad (social interaction, communication, repetitive behavior) is itself a socially defined clinical category; the assigned reading (K&R ch.9 / Stangor ch.6) and the session page's "Check Yourself" quiz cover **attachment（依恋）** — Ainsworth's four patterns (secure, ambivalent/insecure-resistant, avoidant/insecure-avoidant, disorganized) via the Strange Situation — but, notably, **no attachment data appear anywhere in this lecture's own slide deck** (see §6).

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- Piaget's stage-onset ages are systematically too conservative: the lecture's own "Criticism of Piaget" slide states children achieve many of his milestones — including object permanence — earlier than his norms claim, once looking-time rather than manual-search measures are used.
- The A-not-B error is presented here alongside frontal-lobe maturation data, suggesting it may be less about a missing *concept* of permanence and more about an immature capacity to inhibit a previously rewarded reach — i.e., the boundary of the sensorimotor stage may really be an executive-function boundary layered on top of an earlier concept.
- The false-belief age shift (3 vs. 5 years) is measured with a *verbal* task carrying inhibitory and linguistic demands; this is the identical "looking vs. acting" lesson rerun one more time, and the lecture does not show (though does not deny) that nonverbal versions might push the age earlier.
- The autism theory-of-mind account is bounded by its own control condition: the deficit is selective to *mental* content (false belief), not a general deficit in reasoning about any outdated representation (false picture) — so "theory-of-mind deficit" cannot be read as "generally worse at this kind of task."
- The innate-number-sense claim is bounded to small sets (≤3); the Pirahã data show it does not extend to differentiating larger exact quantities without a cultural counting system.
- **Gap between reading and lecture**: the assignment's note that this session covers "nature/nurture in attachment (Harlow, Bowlby/Ainsworth strange situation)" describes the *assigned reading and session-page quiz*, not this lecture's slides — the 42-slide deck contains no Harlow, Bowlby, or Strange-Situation study material at all. This note represents that honestly rather than inventing an evidence chain the lecture did not present.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. If I had to prove to a non-psychologist that infants know more than they can show, I would cite the well-A/well-B footage: the same 7-month-old, at the same moment, gazes at the correct well while his hand uncovers the wrong one. The persuasive move is that one channel (gaze) and another (reach) disagree within a single infant at a single instant — "the baby just doesn't get it yet" cannot explain a live disagreement between the baby's own eyes and hand.
2. Rebuttal to "toddlers eventually find hidden toys because they learn *how* to search, not because they learn objects still exist": the NIRS data do show reaching success tracks frontal-lobe maturation, so search skill matters — but Baillargeon's looking-time studies show violation-of-expectation responses to an "impossible" event *months before* infants can pass the manual test, in infants who cannot yet search successfully. A pure motor-learning account predicts no dissociation between looking and reaching; the data show one.
3. Transfer: if a 3-year-old fails the verbal Sally-Anne task, this lecture's logic predicts she does **not** necessarily lack all insight into other minds — a lower-demand, nonverbal (looking-time) version of the same false-belief scenario should reveal earlier sensitivity, exactly mirroring the object-permanence story: task format, not the underlying concept, sets the observed age boundary.

## 9. Open Questions
- Is the A-not-B error best explained by (a) genuine conceptual immaturity, (b) immature prefrontal inhibitory control (the NIRS-supported reading), or (c) reaching itself being a learned motor habit competing with a fresher memory trace? The lecture leans on both (a)-style Piaget language and (b)-style neuroimaging without adjudicating between them.
- How early does *explicit* (not just looking-time-inferred) false-belief reasoning genuinely emerge, and why does the verbal task cluster so tightly around age 4 across many replications (per Wellman et al.'s meta-analysis, referenced but not detailed on these slides)?
- Where exactly is the boundary of the "innate" small-number sense — fixed at 3 items, or gradable, and does it differ across the auditory and visual modalities tested?
- Attachment (Harlow, Bowlby, Ainsworth's Strange Situation) is assigned reading and appears on the session page's self-check quiz but has no evidence chain in this lecture itself — worth revisiting if a later Emotion/Social lecture returns to it with actual study data.
