# Lec 04 — The Brain II

> One sentence: every claim in [[Lec03-Brain-I]] about localization rides entirely on a research method, and every method buys spatial precision by giving up temporal precision (or vice versa) and buys causal proof by giving up scale and safety (or vice versa).

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: how do scientists actually find out what a piece of brain tissue does — and what does each available method let you conclude versus merely suggest?
- Why it isn't obvious: a colorful fMRI picture of a lit-up brain region *looks* like direct proof that "this is the empathy area" or "this is the fear center," so the naive intuition is that brighter-means-more-involved and involved-means-necessary. Both inferential leaps are unjustified without knowing which method produced the picture.
- What gap from the previous lecture leads here: [[Lec03-Brain-I]] built its entire case for localization (Gage, Tan, split-brain) on lesions and closed by promising to "take up the methods systematically (lesion, TMS, EEG/MEG, PET/fMRI) and their resolution tradeoffs" — this lecture is that promise kept, organized around a single two-axis chart (spatial resolution × temporal resolution) that every technique in §3 gets placed on.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Spatial vs. temporal resolution（空间分辨率 vs. 时间分辨率） | How precisely a method can localize *where* a signal comes from, versus how precisely it can time *when* it happened; every recording method trades one for the other | Not two independent virtues you can maximize together — the lecture's resolution chart shows methods clustering along a tradeoff curve, not scattered freely |
| Causal (necessity) vs. correlational method（因果/必要性方法 vs. 相关性方法） | Lesion and TMS remove or disrupt tissue and ask what stops working — they show a region is *needed*; EEG/MEG/PET/fMRI record activity during a task — they show a region is *involved*, not that it's needed | Involvement ("lights up during X") is routinely over-read as necessity; only damage/disruption methods license a necessity claim, and invasiveness (PET's radioactive tracer) is not the same axis as causality |
| Structure vs. function imaging（结构成像 vs. 功能成像） | CT/MRI/DTI image anatomy — what tissue and connections exist; EEG/MEG/PET/fMRI image physiology — what the tissue is doing right now | A structural scan (e.g., an MRI showing intact hippocampus) says nothing about whether that hippocampus is functioning normally during a task |
| Subtraction method（减法/差异法） | To isolate the brain correlate of one cognitive step, contrast two tasks that differ *only* in that step and subtract the images (e.g., generate-a-use-for-a-word minus read-the-word-aloud isolates semantic association) | Depends on "pure insertion" — the assumption that adding a cognitive step doesn't change how the other steps are carried out; if that assumption fails, the subtracted map is contaminated |
| BOLD signal（血氧水平依赖信号，Blood-Oxygen-Level-Dependent） | fMRI does not measure neurons firing; it measures the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin, because deoxyhemoglobin is more magnetically sensitive, and local blood flow *overshoots* local oxygen use during activation | Not a direct read of neural activity — it is a slow, vascular proxy for it, which is exactly why fMRI's temporal resolution (~6 sec) is far worse than the neurons' own (~10 msec) |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Lesion studies — causal but uncontrolled
- **Design**: exploit naturally occurring tissue loss — stroke/CVA (blood flow disrupted), hypoxia (oxygen loss), tumors, degenerative disorders (Alzheimer's, Huntington's, Parkinson's, Korsakoff's), or planned epilepsy resection — and compare the patient's behavior before/after or against controls.
- **Result**: dramatic, clean dissociations when they occur — Phineas Gage, Tan, H.M., prosopagnosia（面孔失认症，face-blindness with everything else intact); also *counterintuitive* deficits such as blindsight（盲视，cortically blind patients who can still localize objects they report not seeing) and category-specific deficits (losing knowledge of one class of objects while others survive); and clean double dissociations — declarative memory（陈述性记忆，"knowing that"）depends on hippocampus while procedural memory（程序性记忆，"knowing how"）depends on basal ganglia, each spared when the other is damaged. A high-resolution MRI reanalysis of Tan's (Leborgne's) preserved brain found the lesion was in fact extensive, including damage to the arcuate fasciculus / superior longitudinal fasciculus, not a small, clean cut (Dronkers et al., 2007) — updating Lec03's account of Broca's case with better structural imaging.
- **Inference**: lesions sit at the "necessity" end of the causal axis — this is the *only* category of method in the lecture that can prove a region is required for a function, because you are literally removing the candidate cause and watching the effect disappear. On the resolution chart, naturally occurring and induced lesions occupy large spatial extents (map/brain-sized, not neuron-sized) and an essentially unbounded time axis (the "lesion" persists for the rest of the patient's life) — so lesions are the wrong tool for asking "when, in the first 300 msec after a word appears, does semantic access happen?" and the right tool for asking "can this person do X at all without this tissue?"
- **Weakness**: damage is extensive, hard to localize precisely, and not experimentally controlled — nature chooses the cut, so it rarely respects functional boundaries; enormous individual variability (case vs. group-study tradeoff, echoing Lec03's n=1 problem); nearby systems get injured together, confounding which structure did the work; the brain degenerates, recovers, and compensates after the injury, so what you test is not the intact system minus one part; and a lesion study can at best offer a *limited* view of how the healthy brain normally operates, since it studies a reorganized brain.

### Stimulation — electrical and TMS
- **Design**: Penfield (1940s) pioneered direct electrical stimulation of exposed cortex in epilepsy patients during surgery, applying current through electrodes to see what the patient reports or does; the modern non-invasive descendant is transcranial magnetic stimulation（经颅磁刺激，TMS): a figure-8 coil generates a magnetic field that passes through the skull and induces current, making neurons fire (or, at low frequency, suppressing them).
- **Result**: Penfield's method saw only limited use (invasive, only in surgical candidates). TMS spatial resolution is about 1.0–1.5 cm²; low-frequency stimulation (<1 Hz) suppresses neural activity, high-frequency (>1 Hz) enhances it — e.g., temporal-lobe stimulation speeds up picture naming; TMS is also used for motor mapping and, experimentally, as treatment (low-frequency stimulation of auditory cortex reduces auditory hallucinations in schizophrenia).
- **Inference**: like lesions, TMS is a causal/necessity method — it directly perturbs a candidate region and reads out the behavioral consequence — but it is reversible, non-invasive, and can be delivered to healthy volunteers, which lesion studies cannot. Its resolution profile sits close to lesions in spatial grain (cm-scale, "map"-sized) but is far better in time (a single pulse, not a permanent state), so TMS is the right tool for "is this region *causally* involved in this task, right now" and the wrong tool for reaching anything more than ~2 cm below the scalp (deep structures like hippocampus or amygdala are out of reach because the magnetic field weakens with depth).
- **Weakness**: spread of activation beyond the intended target, mild headache, and — most importantly — the same 2 cm depth ceiling that rules out most of the structures (limbic, basal ganglia) that matter most for the higher-cognition questions this lecture ends on.

### Structural recording — CT, MRI, DTI
- **Design**: image anatomy only, not activity. CT (computed tomography) and MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) both image static tissue, with MRI giving much sharper gray/white-matter contrast; diffusion tensor imaging（弥散张量成像，DTI) measures the directional movement of water molecules at the micron level to reconstruct white-matter tracts, since myelin creates a strong barrier that confines diffusion along the length of an axon ("diffusion anisotropy（各向异性扩散）").
- **Result**: MRI resolves degenerative disease anatomically — Huntington's and Alzheimer's both show visible tissue loss (enlarged ventricles, cortical/hippocampal atrophy) compared to a healthy older brain; Parkinson's shows visible pallor of the substantia nigra post-mortem and reduced striatal FDOPA uptake in vivo, scaling with clinical severity (normal → moderate → severe); DTI tractography reconstructs whole white-matter pathways in vivid color-coded 3-D; a between-groups DTI study found reduced anisotropy near temporo-parietal white matter in dyslexic readers versus controls, and anisotropy in that same tract correlated with reading score across individuals (Klingberg et al., 2000).
- **Inference**: structural methods have excellent spatial resolution but *no* temporal resolution at all, because they are not measuring a process — they are measuring a fixed structure. This makes them the right tool for "is there a physical anatomical difference between groups" (e.g., dyslexia vs. controls, Parkinson's vs. normal) and the wrong tool for any question about *when* or *whether* a region is currently doing something — a person's DTI tract map looks the same whether they are reading a word or asleep.
- **Weakness**: an anatomical correlation (thinner tract in poor readers) is silent on causality — does the anatomy cause the reading difficulty, does experience with reading shape the anatomy, or does a third factor produce both? The learning-driven changes shown next in this lecture (taxi drivers, jugglers, exam-crammers) prove structure itself is *plastic*, undercutting any assumption that a structural difference must be an innate cause.

### Structural change with learning — the plasticity aside
- **Design**: repeated MRI scans of the same people before/after a training period: medical students before and after cramming for the German preliminary medical exam (voxel-based morphometry of hippocampus, Draganski et al., 2006); London taxi drivers with varying years of navigation experience (hippocampal volume via structural MRI, Maguire et al., 2000); healthy adults before/after learning a three-ball juggling routine over three months (structural scan of motion-processing area hMT/V5, Draganski et al., 2004).
- **Result**: hippocampal gray matter increased with exam study; posterior hippocampal volume correlated positively with months of taxi-driving experience while anterior hippocampal cross-sectional area correlated negatively with the same measure; jugglers showed measurable gray-matter change in hMT/V5 after training.
- **Inference**: this is a structural (not functional) recording, yet it discriminates a real question — brain anatomy is not fixed after development, so a structural difference between two groups of adults can be a *consequence* of experience rather than a pre-existing cause. It licenses "the adult brain's grey matter is a moving target," a background condition anyone reading a structural difference (like the dyslexia DTI result above) must keep in mind.
- **Weakness**: the taxi-driver design as presented is correlational and (in its original form) cross-sectional rather than a true before/after scan of the same people, so reverse causation (people with certain hippocampi self-select into taxi driving) cannot be fully excluded from that study alone; the exam-study and juggling designs are stronger because they scan the same individuals before and after.

### Electrical/magnetic function recording — EEG/ERP and MEG
- **Design**: electroencephalography（脑电图，EEG) places 16–256 surface electrodes on the scalp and measures voltage changes produced by neurons; because a single electrode needs a few hundred thousand neurons firing in synchrony to detect anything, raw EEG reflects *overall* ongoing brain activity, not a specific stimulus. Event-related potentials（事件相关电位，ERP) solve this by time-locking the recording to stimulus onset and averaging across many trials, canceling noise and isolating the response to that stimulus. Magnetoencephalography（脑磁图，MEG) instead detects the tiny magnetic fields active neurons produce, using superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) sensitive enough to register signals 100 million times smaller than the earth's own magnetic field.
- **Result**: reading sentences whose last word is either normal, semantically odd ("He spread the warm bread with socks"), or printed with a physical/font deviance produces two dissociable ERP components — an N400（负波，~400 msec) for semantic deviance and a P560（正波，~560 msec) for physical/perceptual deviance. MEG of silent word-reading resolves the response down to the millisecond, showing the spatial sequence of activation across the ventral surface starting near stimulus onset (Dale & Halgren, 2000).
- **Inference**: EEG/ERP and MEG sit at the opposite end of the resolution tradeoff from lesions/CT/MRI — both have millisecond-scale temporal resolution (~10 msec), matching the actual speed of neural communication established anatomically in [[Lec03-Brain-I]] (~10 msec per synaptic transmission) — but poor-to-modest spatial resolution, because both are inverse problems: many different underlying source configurations can produce the same surface pattern ("where is (are) the signal generator(s)?"). MEG has somewhat better spatial resolution than EEG because magnetic fields, unlike electrical ones, pass through skull and scalp without distortion. The N400/P560 dissociation itself is evidence that semantic-anomaly detection and low-level perceptual-mismatch detection are separate, separately timed processes — the kind of claim only a millisecond-resolution method could support. This makes EEG/MEG the right tool for "in what order and at what latency do processing stages happen" and the wrong tool for "exactly which gyrus generated this."
- **Weakness**: EEG/ERP require many trials to average away noise (a single trial is unusable); MEG can only detect neurons whose orientation is roughly parallel to the skull surface, missing activity from perpendicularly oriented sources; MEG equipment costs upward of $1 million.

### Metabolic function recording — PET
- **Design**: positron emission tomography（正电子发射断层扫描，PET) exploits the fact first observed by Angelo Mosso in the 1880s (a device recording forearm vs. head/brain pulsations while a monk solved arithmetic or heard church bells) that increased regional neural activity draws increased local blood flow. PET injects a radioactive tracer and measures where cerebral blood flow (rCBF) rises, using the subtraction method: e.g., the "verb generation" task (see a noun, silently generate a use for it) is contrasted against a hierarchy of control tasks (fixation only → passively viewing words → repeating words aloud → generating uses) so that each subtraction isolates one added cognitive operation — passive sensory/word-level coding, then articulatory/motor coding, then semantic association and selection for action (Petersen and colleagues' hierarchical lexical-access paradigm).
- **Result**: subtracting "hearing/reading/speaking words" from "generating a use for a word" isolates left-frontal activity specific to semantic association ("Seeing a word" = board-condition minus fixation, etc.); a separate PET study contrasting globally- versus locally-directed attention to hierarchical letter stimuli (a big "S" built of small "F"s) found distinct hemisphere-weighted activations for global vs. local attention respectively (Fink et al., 1996) — a direct functional-imaging echo of the split-brain global/local finding from [[Lec03-Brain-I]]. Striatal dopamine release was shown to track video-game performance level (Koepp et al., 1998), and PET (FDOPA tracer) visibly distinguishes normal striatal dopamine uptake from the markedly reduced uptake of Parkinson's disease.
- **Inference**: PET has good spatial resolution (5–10 mm — worse than fMRI but better than EEG/MEG) and poor temporal resolution (each scan integrates activity over roughly a minute, since it depends on tracer accumulation) — so PET is the right tool for localizing *where* a well-defined, sustained cognitive operation lives, and the wrong tool for tracking any process that unfolds faster than about a minute. Critically, PET (like all recording methods here) is correlational, not causal: elevated blood flow during a task shows involvement, not necessity — the necessity claim still requires a lesion or TMS result on the same region.
- **Weakness**: extremely invasive (radioactive tracer injection), rare and expensive (~$3 million equipment, ~$700K/year maintenance), and the subtraction logic assumes pure insertion, which may not hold.

### Metabolic function recording — fMRI (BOLD)
- **Design**: functional MRI（功能磁共振成像，fMRI) uses the same magnet as structural MRI but tracks the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin, because deoxyhemoglobin is more magnetically detectable — the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) effect. A visual/cognitive stimulus is presented to a participant in the scanner and the resulting BOLD time-course is recorded voxel by voxel.
- **Result**: the BOLD response to a single stimulus shows an initial *small dip* below baseline (reflecting local oxygen consumption by the just-activated neurons — too small a signal to reliably use) followed by a much larger *rise* over about 10–15 sec (reflecting arterial blood flow overcompensating and delivering more oxygen than the tissue is currently using) — it is this delayed overshoot, not the immediate oxygen use, that produces the measurable, positive BOLD signal researchers actually analyze.
- **Inference**: fMRI's spatial resolution (2–3 mm) is the best of any method in this lecture, but its temporal resolution (~6 sec to resolve the hemodynamic peak) is far worse than the brain's own ~10 msec neural timescale — a direct instance of the resolution tradeoff that structures the whole lecture. fMRI is therefore the right tool for asking "which precise piece of tissue is involved," the wrong tool for "in what millisecond order did processing happen," and — like PET — a correlational, not causal, method: activation is evidence of involvement, never of necessity.
- **Weakness**: expensive (~$4 million equipment, $300–1,000 per scan) though non-invasive, widely available, and applicable to a huge range of cognitive tasks — but still fundamentally correlational; a lit-up region proves nothing about whether that region is required, only that its blood flow changed during the task.

### Application: from method to higher-cognition question
- **Design**: two examples of pushing these tools onto questions with real stakes. (1) Empathy: defined operationally as "observation or imagination of another person in a particular emotional state automatically activates a representation of that state in the observer" (Preston & de Waal, 2002); fMRI contrasts "feeling pain oneself" vs. "seeing a partner receive pain" (Pain > No-Pain, for both "self" and "other"), and a second manipulation uses an ultimatum-style trust/fairness game (proposer/responder split a sum of money; rejection = no money for anyone; modal offer ~50%; low offers ~20% face about a 50% chance of rejection) before measuring empathic brain response to a fair vs. an unfair partner's pain (Singer et al., 2006). (2) Vegetative state: a 23-year-old woman, 5 months after severe traumatic brain injury from a road accident, unresponsive but with preserved sleep-wake cycles, was asked (via fMRI) to imagine playing tennis and to imagine walking through her house, room by room, starting at the front door; her activation pattern (supplementary motor area for tennis imagery; parahippocampal/posterior-parietal/premotor areas for spatial-navigation imagery) was compared to 12 healthy volunteers doing the same imagery tasks (Owen et al., 2006).
- **Result**: ACC and bilateral insula activate both when a person feels pain and when they watch their partner feel pain (self/other overlap, "Pain > No Pain" for both). This "other" activation is reduced for an unfair partner; men (but not women) additionally show increased nucleus accumbens activity when watching an unfair partner in pain versus a fair one, alongside stronger self-reported desire for revenge. The vegetative-state patient's imagery-driven activation was statistically indistinguishable from the healthy controls' pattern for both the tennis and the navigation task.
- **Inference**: this is exactly what the chained methods buy you — fMRI's spatial resolution is precise enough to show that the *same* pain-processing regions (ACC, insula) activate for self and other (evidence for a shared neural code underlying empathy, not just abstract knowledge that "pain is bad") and, in the clinical case, sensitive enough to detect a specific, controls-matched activation pattern in a patient who could produce no behavioral evidence of covert command-following — a functional-imaging analog of the split-brain logic in [[Lec03-Brain-I]] ("the mouth's report ≠ everything the brain is doing"), here used to argue this vegetative-state patient retained covert command-following ability despite showing no observable behavior.
- **Weakness**: the vegetative-state result is n=1 with no prior baseline testing, and a positive fMRI response to instructions does not settle *how much* awareness is present, only that some goal-directed, controls-matched processing occurred — the interpretive gap is the same one blindsight raised in Lec03: information can be processed without being reportable, so a "yes" answer here is compatible with several different pictures of the patient's subjective state. The Terry Schiavo case (cardiac arrest 1990 → persistent vegetative state → husband petitioned to remove her feeding tube in 1998, opposed by her parents → tube removed then reinserted in 2001 → years of court battles and federal legislation signed by President Bush → tube disconnected March 18, 2005, death March 31, 2005) is included on the slides to show why getting this distinction right — and being honest about the actual evidential limits of an imaging result — has real legal and ethical consequences.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| A causal method (lesion, TMS) is required to claim a region is *necessary* for a function | A correlational method (EEG/MEG/PET/fMRI) showing activation is sufficient to claim a region "does" a function | None of the correlational studies in this lecture (verb generation, empathy, global/local attention) license a necessity claim on their own — the lecture's own framing insists activation ⇒ involvement only, echoing Lec03's Broca lesson that even a clean lesion effect doesn't prove a region computes (vs. merely relays) the function |
| BOLD signal increase should be impossible during activation, since active neurons *consume* oxygen (which should transiently lower the oxy:deoxy ratio) | BOLD signal increase is the normal, measured response, because arterial blood flow overcompensates and delivers far more oxygen than is used | The two-phase time-course itself: a real but small initial dip (too small to reliably detect) is followed by the large, measurable overshoot that all standard fMRI analyses report — the "puzzle" slide's own answer |

Otherwise: — the lecture is organized around the resolution-tradeoff chart rather than a substantive theoretical dispute; most of §3 is method taxonomy, not competing explanations of a single phenomenon.

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: single-neuron firing (patch clamp, single-unit recording — appears only as the fine end of the resolution chart, not otherwise used this lecture), synchronized population activity underlying EEG, magnetic fields from active neurons (MEG), regional cerebral blood flow and oxy/deoxyhemoglobin ratios (PET, fMRI), white-matter anisotropy (DTI), striatal dopamine (Parkinson's, video-game study).
- Cognitive/information-processing: the hierarchical subtraction logic itself is a claim about processing stages — passive sensory coding → articulatory/motor coding → semantic association/selection for action; the N400 vs. P560 dissociation as evidence for functionally distinct semantic- vs. perceptual-anomaly detectors; global vs. local visual attention as distinct operations recruiting different hemispheres.
- Behavioral: picture-naming speed changes under TMS; reading score correlating with white-matter anisotropy; ultimatum-game offer acceptance/rejection rates; imagined tennis-playing / house-navigation as a covert behavioral proxy in an unresponsive patient.
- Social/cultural: empathy and fairness studies frame brain imaging as evidence in debates about morality and gender differences in revenge motivation; the Terry Schiavo case shows imaging (and its absence at the bedside) entering courtrooms and federal legislation — a science-becomes-social-technology arc structurally similar to phrenology in [[Lec03-Brain-I]], except here the underlying methods (structural CT/MRI, fMRI) are far better validated than phrenology's skull-bump reading.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- **A structural difference is not a functional explanation.** DTI shows dyslexic readers have lower anisotropy in a specific tract, and the taxi-driver/juggler/med-student studies show that very tract- and volume-level anatomy is plastic with experience — so an observed structural difference between two groups could be cause, consequence, or a shared third factor, and structural imaging alone cannot decide which.
- **Correlational imaging cannot be upgraded to a necessity claim by turning up the color scale.** No amount of PET/fMRI activation, however large or reliable, substitutes for a lesion or TMS result; the two evidence types answer different questions and the lecture keeps them on separate branches of its own outline.
- **The resolution tradeoff is closer to a hard constraint than an engineering gap to be solved.** Every method on the chart sits on essentially one tradeoff curve (EEG/MEG: great time, poor space; PET/fMRI: decent-to-great space, poor time; lesions/TMS: large-to-moderate space, unbounded-to-brief time) — no single method in this lecture achieves both millisecond timing and millimeter localization simultaneously.
- **Depth ceilings**: TMS cannot reach more than ~2 cm below the scalp, ruling out most subcortical/limbic structures that matter for emotion and memory — exactly the structures most relevant to the empathy and vegetative-state applications at the end of the lecture.
- **A positive imaging result in a non-communicative patient is a lower bound on covert processing, not a settled statement about the patient's subjective experience** — the same reportability gap that made split-brain confabulation and blindsight informative in Lec03, and here in Owen et al.'s single case.

[[Lec05-Vision-I]] reuses the hemifield/retina→LGN→V1 anatomy and will lean on these same recording methods (especially fMRI) as its main evidence tool without re-explaining BOLD, so this lecture is the methods foundation for the rest of the visual-system unit; [[Lec06-Vision-II]] and [[Lec07-Attention]] (the Fink et al. global/local-attention PET study bridges directly into attention) both inherit these same recording methods.
- One concrete real-life example (my own): a hospital planning epilepsy surgery might first get a structural MRI/DTI to see the anatomy and rule out major white-matter tracts near the target, then use scalp EEG to time-lock and localize the patient's seizure onset (temporal resolution to catch the seizure's spread in milliseconds), and finally use an intraoperative Wada-style test or direct cortical stimulation (Penfield's method) before cutting — deliberately chaining a structural, a temporal, and a causal method because no single one of them answers "is this exact piece of tissue safe to remove."

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that brain scans alone don't prove causation, I would cite the fact that PET/fMRI activation during verb generation or empathy tasks is explicitly framed in the lecture as correlational — the *only* methods that prove necessity are lesion or TMS, and none of the empathy/attention imaging studies included a lesion or TMS component.
2. Rebuttal to "of course fMRI shows what a region does, it lights up during the task": that inference confuses correlation with necessity — the region's blood flow changing during the task shows involvement, not that removing the region would impair the task; Broca's area itself (Lec03, and the 2007 MRI reanalysis of Tan's actual lesion extent) shows why this matters — a region can be reliably active without being solely dedicated to the function in question.
3. Transfer: if a clinical team wants to know whether a comatose patient can still understand spoken commands, the Owen et al. (2006) logic predicts they should give an fMRI mental-imagery task (e.g., "imagine playing tennis" vs. "imagine walking through your house") and compare the patient's activation pattern to healthy controls doing the same task — a positive, controls-matched result is evidence of covert command-following, though (per §6) it is not proof of a particular level of conscious awareness.

## 9. Open Questions
- The lecture explains *why* BOLD signal rises (arterial overcompensation) but does not explain why the vascular system overcompensates so much in the first place, or whether the size of the overshoot itself carries information about the intensity of neural activity.
- No method here combines millisecond timing with millimeter localization; the lecture doesn't address multimodal approaches (e.g., simultaneous EEG-fMRI, or TMS-EEG) that might triangulate both axes at once.
- For the vegetative-state case: what is the appropriate evidential threshold — how many controls-matched imagery tasks, how many trials — before a clinical team should say a patient is aware, and how often does the Owen et al. result replicate across other vegetative-state patients? Not addressed here.
- Given that adult brain structure changes measurably within three months of learning to juggle, how much of any structural difference reported anywhere in psychology (including the dyslexia DTI result) should we now default to treating as possibly experience-driven rather than a pre-existing trait?
