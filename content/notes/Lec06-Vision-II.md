# Lec 06 — Vision II

> One sentence: seeing a shape is not the same computation as knowing what it is or whose it is — object recognition splits into a **perceptual** stage (build a percept from sense data), a **semantic** stage (attach meaning to that percept), and, for faces specifically, an **identity** vs. **expression** split, each dissociable by brain damage and each shaped by a mix of built-in bias and slowly acquired, experience-dependent expertise.

> Note on scope: the syllabus description for this session promised shape/motion/depth/color-constancy and the binding problem. The slide deck actually delivered (`lec06_vis2.pdf`) and the session page's chapter list (Introduction to Recognition, Problems with Object Recognition, Recognizing Faces and Prosopagnosia, Development of Facial Recognition, Word Recognition and Alexia, Predisposition to Facial Recognition, Race and Facial Recognition, Recognizing Fear) instead cover **object agnosia, category-specific knowledge, face recognition (identity + expression), and word recognition (alexia)**. This note follows the actual material; see §9 for where the binding problem resurfaces.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: once a shape has been extracted from the retinal image (Vision I's job), how does the brain turn that shape into "I see a chair," "that's Obama," or "that word says THIS" — and can these steps fail independently of each other?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection presents recognition as one instantaneous, unified act ("I just see that it's a face"), so the natural assumption is a single recognition process. The lecture's agnosia and prosopagnosia patients show this is false — patients can lose the ability to *build* a percept while their senses and language are intact, or lose the ability to *attach meaning* to a percept they can otherwise copy perfectly, or lose faces specifically while objects survive.
- Gap from the previous lecture: [[Lec05-Vision-I]] established the feedforward hierarchy (retina → LGN → V1 → V2/V4/MT) that produces a coherent low-level percept. This lecture asks what happens *after* V1 has done its job — how percept becomes identity, category, and meaning — and shows that this later stage is not one box but several dissociable ones.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Agnosia（失认症） | Modality-specific failure to recognize a stimulus, not explained by sensory, attentional, or linguistic deficits (patient can see, just can't recognize) | Not blindness — patients can often draw or point to the stimulus; not aphasia — the failure is modality-bound |
| Apperceptive vs. associative agnosia（统觉性 vs. 联想性失认症） | Apperceptive = can't even construct a conscious percept from sense data (fails at copying/matching shapes, right hemisphere); associative = percept is intact (can copy/match) but stripped of meaning (left hemisphere) | Easy to collapse into "can't recognize things" generically — the diagnostic test is whether **copying** fails (apperceptive) or only **naming/definition** fails (associative) |
| Category-specific knowledge deficit（范畴特异性知识缺陷） | Brain damage can selectively remove knowledge of one semantic category (e.g., living things) while sparing another (e.g., manufactured objects), or vice versa | Not a simple "amount of damage" effect; the double dissociation is by category, tracking the sensory/motor channel the category is normally learned through |
| Fusiform Face Area (FFA)（梭状回面孔区） | A fusiform-cortex region whose fMRI signal rises selectively when faces (vs. objects, scrambled faces, houses, hands) are shown | Not proof of an innate "face module" by itself — see the expertise-hypothesis challenge in §4 |
| Configural / holistic face processing（构型/整体面孔加工） | Faces are read as one indivisible spatial configuration (relative position of eyes/nose/mouth), not as a sum of independently identified parts | Not the same as "faces are just harder objects" — the whole-vs-part advantage is disproportionately large for faces relative to a matched non-face control (houses) |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Apperceptive vs. associative agnosia (Lissauer 1890; case figures from Farah's collection; Landis et al. 1982)
- **Design**: case descriptions of agnosic patients tested on (i) free-hand copying of simple shapes and letters/numbers, (ii) forced-choice shape/line matching, and (iii) reading of ambiguous letter strings.
- **Result**: patient E.S. copying a simple geometric figure (triangle-with-inset-square) produced only an unrecognizable scribble; patient Mr. S., shown pairs of rectangles matched for area, could not judge same/shape-different except for the most extreme pair, and erred even there; on a shape-matching task with four choices he also failed broadly. Separately, patient X (Landis et al. 1982) was shown the word "THIS" in a clear block-letter font and **consistently read it as "7415."**
- **Inference**: because Mr. S. and E.S. fail at pure copying and matching — tasks that require no semantic knowledge at all — their deficit must sit *before* meaning is even reachable: they cannot construct a stable conscious percept from the sense data (apperceptive, associated with right-hemisphere damage in Lissauer's scheme). Patient X's THIS→7415 substitution shows a different failure mode: the shapes are evidently perceived well enough to be copied/matched (an associative-stage patient can do this), but the **assignment of identity/category** (letter vs. digit) to an already-correct percept can misfire — demonstrating that "seeing the shape correctly" and "knowing what kind of thing it is" are separable computations.
- **Weakness**: these are single/small case reports, not group experiments; the right-hemisphere/left-hemisphere assignment is a generalization across cases rather than a demonstrated mechanism in any one patient; no manipulation, only description, so causal specificity (does the lesion really *cause* exactly this and nothing else) is not directly tested here.

### Category-specific knowledge deficits and the sensory/motor organization of concepts
- **Design**: neuropsychological case series testing patients' ability to define and word-picture-match items from different semantic categories (manufactured objects vs. foods/animals; body parts vs. musical instruments; large outdoor objects vs. small manipulable objects). Separately, fMRI while healthy subjects named line drawings or read words for animals vs. tools.
- **Result**: a double dissociation — some patients can define/match manufactured objects but not foods/animals; others show the reverse pattern (and finer splits recur: intact on body parts but impaired on musical instruments; better on large outdoor objects than small manipulable ones). In the fMRI data, naming animals activated visual cortex more than naming tools did; naming tools activated visual-motion and hand-action areas more than naming animals did.
- **Inference**: the double dissociation rules out a single unitary "object knowledge" store — losing one category while keeping another requires separable substrates. The fMRI dissociation supplies the organizing principle: categories are represented via the sensory/motor channel through which they are predominantly learned — animals via fine visual discrimination, tools via functional/motor interaction (illustrated on the slides by the magpie-vs-scissors diagram: both engage visual regions, but only the tool additionally engages sensorimotor "hand action" cortex). Damage to a modality-specific processing stream therefore removes exactly the categories that lean on it, not categories grouped by an abstract living/nonliving list.
- **Weakness**: "living vs. nonliving" partly confounds with visual complexity/typicality/familiarity, and the finer exceptions (body parts vs. instruments) show the category boundary is not clean; patient samples are small; the fMRI activation differences are correlational, not proof that the region is *necessary* for the category (cf. the necessity-vs-correlation caution from [[Lec03-Brain-I]]).

### Fusiform Face Area selectivity (Kanwisher lab fMRI)
- **Design**: block-design fMRI with multiple independent contrasts on the same candidate region: faces vs. objects, intact faces vs. scrambled faces, faces vs. houses, and 3/4-view faces vs. hands, using both anatomical and functional region-of-interest (ROI) definitions.
- **Result**: one fusiform region shows a consistent, time-locked percent-signal-change spike specifically during face blocks across *every* one of these contrasts — not just faces-vs.-objects, but also intact-vs.-scrambled (rules out low-level image statistics) and faces-vs.-houses/hands (rules out generic "interesting complex stimulus").
- **Inference**: because the same region survives several different control comparisons, each targeting a different alternative explanation (visual complexity, low-level features, general salience), the selectivity is plausibly about face **content** specifically, not an artifact of any single control. This is the empirical anchor for calling the region a face-selective area at all.
- **Weakness**: still a correlational functional signature (activation, not necessity — a lesion or TMS study would be needed to show the region is required); functional-ROI-then-test-more-contrasts risks circularity unless the localizer and the test data are independent (the slides don't specify this); and, per §4, activation selectivity for faces does not by itself decide *why* the region is face-selective (innate module vs. acquired expertise).

### Development of face preference: innate bias plus a narrow, experience-set sensitive period (Cassia, Macchi Turati & Simion 2004; Sugita 2008)
- **Design (Cassia et al. 2004)**: newborns' looking time measured in three paired-preference experiments: (1) upright vs. upside-down face, (2) top-heavy vs. bottom-heavy *non-face* scrambled configuration, (3) upright face vs. a top-heavy non-face configuration.
- **Design (Sugita 2008)**: infant macaques reared with **zero exposure to any face**, human or monkey, for 6–24 months; tested for face preference/discrimination before ever seeing a real face; then given **one month** of exposure to only one species' faces (human or monkey); retested; then followed for over a year with exposure to both species.
- **Result**: Cassia — Exp 1: upright preferred over upside-down (p<.03); Exp 2: top-heavy preferred over bottom-heavy scrambled pattern (p<.001); Exp 3: upright face vs. top-heavy non-face configuration — **no difference** (p=.39). Sugita — before any face exposure, infants already preferred human *and* monkey face photos equally over objects and could discriminate the two species; after one month exposed to only one species, they developed a preference specific to *that* species over objects, a preference that persisted over a year despite later exposure to both species.
- **Inference**: Cassia's null result in Exp 3 shows that newborns' famous "face preference" is largely explained by a domain-general bias toward top-heavy patterns, not a dedicated face detector — an upright non-face pattern draws just as much looking time as an actual face. Sugita shows something innate is nonetheless present (equal preference/discrimination for both species pre-exposure — this could not be learned from experience that never happened) — but *which* species becomes the specialized, lastingly-preferred category is set by a brief window of early experience and does not simply update with later exposure. Together the two studies argue for a hybrid: general perceptual scaffolding at birth, narrowed into face-type-specific expertise during a sensitive period.
- **Weakness**: both measure preference/discrimination via looking time, not full recognition or identification; Cassia's samples are small newborn cohorts; Sugita's design is inherently small-n (ethically constrained primate rearing) and extrapolating from macaque to human face development is an inference, not a direct demonstration.

### Configural/holistic face processing and the expertise hypothesis (Obama part/whole test; face inversion; dog-show judges)
- **Design**: participants judge whether an isolated part (a nose, or a window) belongs to a known individual/building, shown either alone (part condition) or embedded in the whole image (whole condition), separately for faces (Obama) and houses (Obama's house), with percent-correct compared across isolated-part vs. whole-object presentation. Separately: face-inversion-effect (recognition cost for upside-down faces) measured across development, and in adult dog-show judges' experience-dependent inversion effect for *dog* faces.
- **Result**: percent correct rises substantially from part to whole condition for faces (~66%→75%), but is essentially flat for houses (~82%→80%). The face-inversion effect does not reach adult strength until about age 16. Dog-show judges take **about 8 years** of professional experience to develop a face-inversion effect specifically for dog faces.
- **Inference**: the disproportionate whole-object boost for faces (relative to the matched house control) shows faces are read as one indivisible configuration rather than assembled from independently-identified parts — "context helps" alone would predict a similar house boost, which is not observed. That this configural mode takes until age 16 to mature, and can be built from scratch for a *non-face* category (dog faces) given ~8 years of deliberate practice, argues that configural/inversion effects reflect **acquired perceptual expertise**, not an exclusively face-only, hard-wired module — directly challenging a pure domain-specific reading of the FFA data above.
- **Weakness**: houses are an imperfect control (parts and wholes mean something structurally different for a building than a face); the dog-judge finding is reported as a single demonstration without sample size or effect size; the direction of causality (practice creates configural processing, vs. people who already process configurally self-select into face/dog expertise) isn't established by these comparisons.

### Same-race (own-race) face memory advantage and its developmental origin
- **Design**: African-American (AA) and European-American (EA) subjects studied and later discriminated same-race vs. other-race faces (recognition memory, quantified as d′); FFA activation separately compared for same-race vs. other-race faces at two different activation thresholds. Developmentally: Korean children adopted between ages 3–9 by European Caucasian families, compared against French-raised and Korea-raised comparison groups on face memory.
- **Result**: both subject groups discriminate own-race faces better than other-race faces (d′), with a markedly steeper effect in the EA group in the data shown; FFA shows greater percent-signal-change for same-race than other-race faces, robust across both activation-threshold definitions used. The bias is absent at birth, present by 3 months, and the Korean adoptees' face memory matched their European-Caucasian rearing environment (like French-raised children) and was **opposite** to Korea-raised Korean children.
- **Inference**: the adoption result is the key discriminating datum — adoptees share Korean ancestry with the Korea-raised comparison group but not their face-memory pattern, and share European rearing (not ancestry) with the French comparison group whose pattern they *do* match. This pins the cause on exposure history, not genetic/ancestral category, and identifies the "other-race effect" as the same perceptual-expertise mechanism as configural processing generally: the system tunes to whatever population of faces it is most exposed to early, at both a behavioral (d′) and neural (FFA) level.
- **Weakness**: adoption is a quasi-experiment, not randomized; the "not present at birth / present by 3 months" claims rest on looking-time-style paradigms with the same inferential limits noted in the development block above; AA/EA is a coarse binary that may not generalize to other group pairings or to genuinely mixed-exposure childhoods.

### Amygdala and the recognition of fear (rodent lesions; human neuropsychology)
- **Design**: rodent studies lesion the amygdala and observe behavior toward a natural predator cue (a rat, amygdala-lesioned, housed with/exposed to a cat). Human studies test face-expression recognition, across the six universal facial expressions, in patients with amygdala damage, alongside tests of basic face-identity recognition.
- **Result**: amygdala-lesioned rats lose the normal fear/avoidance response to the cat. Human patients with amygdala damage show **intact face-identity recognition** but a selective impairment specifically in recognizing **fearful** expressions.
- **Inference**: the slide deck opens the Faces section with an explicit split — Identity vs. Expression — and this evidence delivers the double dissociation that justifies it: identity recognition survives amygdala damage (so identity is not routed through the amygdala) while fear-expression recognition does not (so at least one specific expression-reading computation is). Combined with the FFA data above (damage/selectivity for identity lives in fusiform cortex), this is a second dissociation inside face processing, on top of the apperceptive/associative and category-specific ones already established.
- **Weakness**: the rodent finding (losing predator avoidance behavior) and the human finding (failing to *recognize* a fearful face in someone else) are different tasks entirely — having/expressing fear vs. reading fear in another face — so the cross-species link is suggestive, not a demonstrated single mechanism; "selective for fear" rests on whatever expression battery was used, and the slide gives no effect sizes or the number of expressions tested.

### Reading as a repurposed visual system (Moving Window experiment; the 1887 "Mr. C" case)
- **Design**: eye-tracking during normal reading, with every letter outside the current fixation replaced by "x" after each saccade (McConkie & Raynor 1975); separately, an 1887 case description ("Mr. C") of a patient who could see, could hear and speak words, could still read numbers, and could still write words correctly to dictation, but had lost the ability to read written words.
- **Result**: adult readers process about 12 letters per fixation before moving their eyes (typical reading speed 200–250 words/minute, recognizing 1 of 50,000–100,000 known words in about 50 thousandths of a second), and in the moving-window manipulation, readers **did not notice** that everything outside that ~12-letter window had been replaced with x's. Mr. C's reading loss was accompanied by fully intact vision, spoken language, number-reading, and writing-to-dictation.
- **Inference**: the moving-window null result shows normal reading extracts detailed letter information from only a narrow foveal/parafoveal span per fixation — most of a line of text is not being read letter-by-letter at any instant, even though it feels seamless. Mr. C's dissociation isolates "reading written words" as its own component, separable from vision (he could see), from spoken language (he could hear and speak), from number symbols (he could read digits), and even from writing (he could still write words he couldn't then read back) — a strikingly selective loss that argues for a specific visual-word pathway rather than a general symbolic or linguistic deficit. Given how recent writing is as a cultural technology (cuneiform ≈3200 BC; mass literacy only after the Gutenberg press, 1450s), this pathway cannot be an evolved, purpose-built module — it must be assembled out of older visual and language circuitry, which is exactly the kind of narrow, learnable, damageable route Mr. C's case implies.
- **Weakness**: "did not notice" in the moving-window study is a subjective report, not an objective detection threshold; Mr. C is an n=1 historical case (1887) with no modern lesion localization, so the claim of a dedicated pathway is an inference from the pattern of sparing, not a shown anatomical fact; the slides themselves omit the (copyrighted) figures that would show where such a pathway sits.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| FFA is a dedicated, largely innate **face module** (domain-specific) | FFA activation reflects domain-general **perceptual expertise** built by years of fine visual discrimination practice, applied most often to faces because everyone is a lifelong face expert | Dog-show judges developing a face-inversion effect for *dog* faces only after ~8 years of professional practice — an exclusively face-specific, hard-wired account does not predict that a non-face category can acquire the same signature through training |
| Category knowledge is stored in one **amodal semantic system**, indifferent to how a concept was learned | Category knowledge is organized by the **sensory/motor channel** used to learn it (vision-heavy for animals, motor/functional-heavy for tools) | The fMRI double dissociation (naming animals recruits visual cortex more; naming tools recruits visual-motion + hand-action cortex more) plus the patient double dissociation (manufactured-objects-only deficits vs. foods/animals-only deficits) — an amodal account predicts no such category-by-modality pairing |
| Face preference at birth reflects a dedicated, face-specific detector (nativist) | Face preference at birth is a general perceptual bias (top-heavy patterns), only later tuned into face-specific expertise by experience | Cassia et al. Exp 3: an upright real face draws no more looking time than a top-heavy *non-face* pattern (p=.39) — yet Sugita shows an innate, pre-experience, species-general face/non-face discrimination also exists; the data support a hybrid (general bias at birth + experience-set specialization), not a clean either/or |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: fusiform face area (FFA) and its selectivity across multiple fMRI contrasts; amygdala for fear-expression reading; posterior cortical lesions causing prosopagnosia; right hemisphere implicated in apperceptive agnosia, left hemisphere in associative agnosia; visual cortex vs. visual-motion/hand-action cortex tracking animal- vs. tool-naming.
- Cognitive/information-processing: two-stage object recognition (percept construction → meaning assignment); configural/holistic vs. part-based face processing; category-specific semantic organization by learning modality; reading's narrow (~12-letter) perceptual span per fixation.
- Behavioral: copying and shape-matching failures in apperceptive agnosia; face-inversion effect and its ~8-year acquisition in dog judges; newborn fixation-time preferences; recognition-memory d′ for own- vs. other-race faces; eye-tracking during reading.
- Social/cultural: the own-race bias's real-world stakes connect directly to the eyewitness-misidentification material in [[Lec10-Memory-I]] (Steven Titus, Donald Thompson); reading is a ~5,000-year-old cultural invention (cuneiform, the printing press) riding on much older visual/language machinery; dog-show judging as a real-world professional-expertise analog to face expertise.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- **The FFA-as-face-module story is contested from within this same lecture.** The dog-show-judge inversion-effect finding is presented right alongside the FFA data, and it is hard to reconcile with a purely innate, face-exclusive module — the slides present both without resolving the tension (see §4, row 1).
- **Newborn "face preference" is over-claimed in casual retellings.** Cassia et al.'s own Exp 3 shows an upright face is no more attractive to a newborn than a matched non-face top-heavy pattern; the popular claim that babies are "born loving faces" outruns this specific data.
- **Category-specific deficits don't respect a clean living/nonliving boundary.** The body-parts-intact/musical-instruments-impaired split shown on the slides is not a living/nonliving split at all, which means the "living vs. manufactured" framing is itself an approximation to whatever the true organizing variable is (visual similarity structure, perhaps, more than biological kind).
- **"Own-race" is a misleading label.** The Korean-adoptee data show the effect tracks *exposure history*, not ancestry — a Korean-ancestry child raised among Caucasian faces develops the Caucasian-typical own-race advantage, not the Korean-typical one.
- **Cross-species claims about fear and the amygdala don't fully transfer.** A rat failing to avoid a cat after amygdala lesion and a human failing to *recognize a fearful face* are different behaviors; the human/rodent bridge here is a plausibility argument, not a shown identical mechanism.
- **The reading case (Mr. C) is a single 19th-century report** with no modern lesion mapping — useful for showing that reading *can* dissociate from vision, speech, and writing, but not for localizing exactly where that dissociation lives.

## 7. Connections
- Prior: [[Lec05-Vision-I]] supplies the feedforward hierarchy (retina→LGN→V1→V2/V4/MT) whose output this lecture's recognition stages operate on; the discussion-session demos for this unit (Dalmatian-in-leaves picture: once you know the dog is there, you always see it, because feedback connections in the visual system outnumber feedforward ones roughly 10:1; and the Clinton/Gore, Bush/Cheney spliced-face illusion, where a wrong face reads as right until you isolate the parts) are the same **top-down / configural** point this lecture makes about agnosia and faces, just demonstrated live rather than through patient data.
- Follow-up: [[Lec07-Attention]]. This lecture treats "recognize the shape, then attach identity/category/expression to it" as if each stage runs on its own dedicated, already-solved channel (an object channel here, a face-identity channel there, a fear-expression channel elsewhere). It never asks how those *separate* channels combine into one bound percept of, say, "my frightened friend's face" rather than a frightened face and a friend's identity floating unattached. That combination question is exactly what [[Lec07-Attention]]'s feature integration theory takes on directly, with the binding problem framed explicitly and illusory conjunctions as its diagnostic evidence.
- One concrete real-life example (my own): I can often recognize a friend from behind, from gait and posture alone, well before I can see their face — a functional/motor-style recognition cue, parallel to the "tools are known through motor interaction" half of the category-specific dissociation above, running on a completely different channel than face-identity recognition.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove object recognition has a perceptual stage separate from a semantic stage, I'd cite Mr. S.'s apperceptive agnosia: he fails at pure shape-copying and matching, tasks that require no knowledge of what anything *means* — so his deficit cannot be "doesn't know what it is," it has to be "can't even build a stable percept of it" in the first place.
2. Rebuttal to "of course newborns love faces, they need their mother": Cassia et al.'s third experiment shows an upright real face gets no more looking time than a scrambled, non-face pattern that merely happens to be top-heavy (p=.39) — the "obvious" face preference is, at minimum, partly explained by a domain-general shape bias, not dedicated face recognition.
3. Transfer: if the expertise hypothesis is right, a lifelong bird-watcher who can rapidly distinguish similar species should, after enough years, show a measurable face-inversion-style effect and elevated fusiform-region activation specifically for bird photographs — a testable prediction paralleling the dog-show-judge finding.

## 9. Open Questions
- The lecture's own two datasets (FFA selectivity vs. the trainable dog-judge inversion effect) leave open whether face-selective cortex is a dedicated module or a general-purpose "expertise system" that faces simply dominate by lifelong practice — an active, unresolved dispute the slides present but do not adjudicate.
- Where exactly the visual-word-recognition pathway implied by Mr. C's dissociation sits anatomically is not shown in this deck (the relevant figures were removed for copyright) — the anatomical story of reading is left incomplete here.
- How much of "category-specific" knowledge loss tracks true biological category vs. confounds like visual complexity or typicality is unresolved by the case data shown.
- How do the separately-established identity (FFA) and expression (amygdala/fear) computations get bound into one unified percept of a specific person's specific emotional expression? Not addressed in this lecture — see [[Lec07-Attention]].
