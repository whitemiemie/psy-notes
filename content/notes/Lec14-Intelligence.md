# Lec 14 — Intelligence

> One sentence: intelligence tests measure something real, stable, and predictive of life outcomes, but the score is the joint product of genes, environment, culture, and the testing situation itself — and none of that licenses claims about *why groups differ*.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: what is intelligence, can a single number (IQ) capture it, and how much of the variation between people is due to genes versus environment?
- Why it isn't obvious: introspection and folk psychology treat "smart" as a single, fixed, mostly-inherited trait you either have or don't — so a test score feels like it's reading off a pre-existing quantity. The lecture complicates every part of that: the *number* (IQ) is a ratio invented for a practical school-placement problem, not a physical measurement; the *trait* fractures under factor analysis into general and specific components that different theorists weight differently; and "fixed by genes" turns out to be a claim about **population variance in a given environment**, not about any one person's ceiling.
- Gap from the previous lecture: [[Lec13-Thinking]] covered reasoning and problem-solving as processes; this lecture asks how people *differ* in those processes, which immediately raises the harder, more socially loaded question of measurement, causation, and fairness — the same nature/nurture logic will resurface with less controversy in [[Lec16-Personality]].

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| IQ（智商，Intelligence Quotient） | A standardized score (mean 100, SD 15) locating a person's test performance relative to an age-matched norm group; originally MA/CA×100 | Not a direct physical measurement of "amount of brain power"; it's a norm-referenced ranking that gets **re-centered** every time the test is renormed |
| g / general intelligence（一般智力因素） | The single factor that explains why performance on many different cognitive subtests is positively correlated (Spearman); in Carroll's three-stratum model, g sits atop 8+ group factors and many specific ones, but still "remains best predictor in real life" per the lecture | Not the same as any one subtest score, and not the same as Gardner's multiple intelligences, which reject a single dominant factor |
| Heritability（遗传率） | The proportion of **variance in a trait within a specific population living in a specific range of environments** that is statistically attributable to genetic differences among those individuals | **Not** a statement about any individual's fixed potential, **not** the proportion of a trait inherited from one's parents, and **not** informative about the cause of a *between-group* mean difference (a 90%-heritable trait can still shift its whole distribution with environment — see height/Japan case in §6) |
| Flynn effect（弗林效应） | The generation-over-generation rise in raw IQ scores (~3 points/decade on Raven's Progressive Matrices), largest on abstract/categorical subtests (Similarities) and smallest on fact-based ones (Vocabulary, Information) | Not evidence that genes changed in 2-3 generations; the lecture treats it as proof that environment/culture can move scores a great deal even though the test is the "same" |
| Stereotype threat（刻板印象威胁） | Performance drop caused by the situational anxiety of possibly confirming a negative stereotype about one's group, present only when the task is framed as diagnostic of the relevant ability | Not a claim that the underlying ability differs; the identical item set produces no gap under a non-diagnostic framing (Steele, 1997) |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Standardized tests predict real-world success (Kuncel & Hezlett, *Science*, 2007)
- **Design**: correlate scores on graduate/professional admissions tests (GRE-T, GRE-S, MCAT, LSAT, GMAT, MAT, PCAT) with career outcomes — licensing-exam pass, faculty ratings, citation count, research productivity, degree completion, GPA.
- **Result**: correlations mostly in the 0.2–0.6 range; tests predict success **better than grades alone**; combining test + grades is best of all. Reference point given in lecture: the sex–height correlation is only 0.4, for calibration of what these numbers mean.
- **Inference**: this is the lecture's opening justification for *why* standardized cognitive testing is used at all — the scores are not arbitrary hoops, they carry real predictive information beyond what transcripts already contain.
- **Weakness**: correlational, not causal; predicts group-level outcomes, explains a modest share of variance (IQ itself is stated elsewhere in the deck to account for only ~25% of variation in life outcomes like income/marriage stability/avoiding jail — personality, education, and culture make up the rest).

### Twin and adoption studies — heritability ≈ 0.50, and why heritability is not fixity
- **Design**: compare IQ correlations across kinship types with differing genetic relatedness and rearing arrangements (identical twins reared together/apart, fraternal twins, siblings reared together/apart, parent–child, foster parent–child, unrelated persons reared together).
- **Result**: correlation rises monotonically with genetic relatedness — lowest for unrelated persons/foster pairs, intermediate for parent–child and siblings, highest for identical twins, and still high for identical twins reared **apart** (bars shown on a 0–1.00 scale, no printed values in the slide, but the ordering is unambiguous). Lecture states IQ heritability is "about .50."
- **Inference**: shared genes predict IQ similarity even when rearing environment is *not* shared (identical twins reared apart still correlate highly), which is the classic argument for a genetic contribution. But the lecture immediately complicates this with the **tomato-plant analogy**: cloned tomato plants grown in poor vs. good soil show heritability = 0 (all variance is environmental, since genotype is identical), while genetically diverse plants in varied soil show heritability > 0. Height is ~90% heritable, yet Japanese men are 3 inches taller in the US than in Japan — proving a highly heritable trait can still shift enormously with environment. **This is the crux the lecture wants understood: heritability quantifies variance-explained within the sampled population/environment range, and says nothing about whether a *between-group* mean gap has a genetic or environmental cause.**
- **Weakness**: twin/adoption designs assume equal environments for MZ and DZ twins (an assumption, not a given); heritability estimates are population- and era-specific and will change if the environmental range studied changes (as the Flynn effect and the Japan/height case both illustrate).

### What the Flynn-effect gains are made of, and what counts as "abstract" is culture-specific
- **Design**: compare where 20th-century IQ gains concentrated across the ten WISC subtests, and separately, give a categorization task (sort food/tools/containers/clothing) to the Kpelle tribe in Liberia.
- **Result**: small gains on Vocabulary, General Knowledge, Arithmetic; large gains on Similarities (e.g., "how are dogs and rabbits alike?" — modern Western answer: "both mammals" [abstract/taxonomic]; older/traditional answer: "you use dogs to hunt rabbits" [functional]). The Kpelle, given the same kind of sorting task, spontaneously used **functional pairings** (potato + knife) and only produced taxonomic groupings when asked "how would a fool do it?"
- **Inference**: the Flynn-effect gains are concentrated exactly on the item type (abstract/taxonomic reasoning) that industrialized, schooled populations get more practice with — and the Kpelle data show that "taxonomic = smarter" is itself a culturally specific default, not a culture-free fact about cognition. Together these push against reading IQ subtests as culture-free measures of a fixed quantity.
- **Weakness**: the Kpelle demonstration is a single ethnographic anecdote in this deck, not a quantified cross-cultural battery; no effect-size or sample size given.

### Early language environment predicts later IQ (Hart & Risley)
- **Design**: recorded one full hour per month for 2.5 years of every word spoken at home between parent and child in 42 families, stratified by socioeconomic status (professional / working-class / welfare); coded and analyzed 1,318 transcripts (~30,000 pages).
- **Result**: words heard per hour — professional families 2,153, working-class 750, welfare 616 (a ~300-word/hour gap between professional and welfare, extrapolated to ~11 million vs. ~3 million words/year); cumulative vocabulary by age 3 — 1,100 (professional) vs. 750 (working-class) vs. 500 (welfare) words. Children's academic success at ages 9–10 correlated strongly with the amount of talk heard at ages 0–3.
- **Inference**: this is the lecture's clearest single-mechanism environmental story — a concrete, measurable input (sheer word count) years before school predicts a measurable output (IQ/achievement), independent of any genetic claim.
- **Weakness**: correlational; SES confounds nearly everything else in these families (nutrition, stress, school quality, parental education) and the study cannot isolate word-count as the active ingredient.

### Genetic and neural mechanisms: COMT, working memory, and processing efficiency
- **Design**: (a) Duncan et al. (*Science*, 2000) — fMRI while subjects perform spatial, verbal, and "circles" reasoning tasks contrasted by item difficulty/g-loading; (b) Gray et al. (*Nature Neuroscience*, 2003) — 3-back working-memory task correlated with Raven's-based fluid intelligence (gF) and BOLD signal in lateral PFC/cerebellum; (c) Egan et al. (PNAS, 2001) — COMT gene val108/158met polymorphism (COMT metabolizes dopamine) related to Wisconsin Card Sorting Test perseverative errors and n-back fMRI activation, val/val (fastest dopamine breakdown) showing the worst WCST performance and val/met the most fMRI activation for a given performance level.
- **Result**: high-g contrasts across very different task types (spatial vs. verbal) activate an overlapping fronto-parietal network — a neural echo of the psychometric positive manifold. Higher gF individuals show *greater* signal change during working-memory maintenance/lure trials. Separately, at matched performance, **lower-IQ individuals show more prefrontal activation than higher-IQ individuals** for the same task — i.e., more neural effort for the same output. The COMT val/met genotype shows an analogous dose relationship: val/val patients/controls perform worst and show most inefficient (highest) prefrontal activation.
- **Inference**: this triangulates a neural-efficiency account of g — a shared fronto-parietal/working-memory system whose *efficiency*, not just raw activity, varies with both psychometric intelligence and specific dopamine-regulating genotype, giving a mechanistic bridge between genes, brain, and the psychometric g factor.
- **Weakness**: single-gene (COMT) effects on a complex polygenic trait like g are necessarily small; "more activation = less efficient" is an inference from a correlational neuroimaging pattern, not a demonstrated causal mechanism; sample sizes in these classic imaging studies are modest (n in the dozens).

### Group IQ gaps track environment/history, not a fixed genetic ceiling
- **Design**: track the Black–White IQ gap in the US across development (infancy → age 24), alongside a natural experiment — children fathered by Black vs. White American GIs stationed in Germany after WWII, all raised by German mothers in the same postwar German environment.
- **Result**: no Black–White gap in infancy; ~4.5-point gap by age 4; the deck reports the gap widening across ages 4–24 at roughly 0.6 points/year (a further figure, "83.4," appears on the slide without explanation of its referent). In the German postwar cohort, children of Black GIs and White GIs raised by German mothers showed **the same IQ** — no gap — despite the gap existing in the contemporaneous US-raised population. Median US household income by race is also reported: White $50,700, Black $32,000, Hispanic $37,800.
- **Inference**: the German study is the discriminating case — it holds "race of father" roughly constant while removing the US racial/socioeconomic/schooling environment, and the gap disappears. This is presented as evidence *against* the between-group-gap-is-genetic hypothesis, consistent with the heritability caveat above: whatever produces the US gap tracks the US social environment (of which the income gap is one marker), not ancestry per se.
- **Weakness**: this is a single historical natural experiment with no stated sample size, matching procedure, or original citation on the slide; the "0.6/year, 83.4" figures are given without enough context in the deck to evaluate.

### Stereotype threat depresses performance, and naming it removes it (Steele; Cohen et al.; Ramirez & Beilock)
- **Design**: Steele (1997) gave Black and White students a difficult verbal GRE-style test framed either as a "laboratory problem-solving exercise" (non-diagnostic) or as a "test of intelligence" (diagnostic); a further condition had students list their race just before a non-diagnostic "laboratory experiment" test. Cohen et al. (Science, 2006) had 7th graders write a brief in-class essay affirming a personally important value (vs. a value important to "someone else") early in the school year. Ramirez & Beilock (Science, 2011) had students do timed mental-arithmetic under manufactured social/evaluative pressure, then spend 10 minutes either sitting quietly, writing about unrelated things, or writing expressively about their test-anxiety, before a postt-test.
- **Result**: under "laboratory experiment" framing, Black and White students scored equally; under "test of intelligence" framing, Black students scored markedly worse (see Fig. 2 on slide 74) — and merely listing race before the non-diagnostic test was enough to reproduce the deficit. Cohen et al.: self-affirmation raised Black students' GPA by 0.30 points relative to controls with no effect on White students, closing **40%** of the achievement gap. Ramirez & Beilock: control (sit quietly) group got 7% *worse*; unrelated-writing group got 7% *better*(per the slide's corrected figure); expressive-writing-about-anxiety group got 4% *better* under pressure.
- **Inference**: the size of a race-linked score gap is not fixed by ability — it can be turned on by a label ("test of intelligence," or simply naming race) and turned off/reduced by framing or brief writing interventions, which is strong evidence that at least part of any observed group gap is a testing-situation artifact rather than a stable capacity difference. The lecture generalizes stereotype threat beyond race: women on math tests, older adults, gay men, and athletic-ability stereotypes all show the same pattern, worse with stronger group identification and higher perceived stakes.
- **Weakness**: effects are on test-day performance under lab-induced pressure, not necessarily on the deeper causes of long-run achievement gaps; the achievement-gap graph itself (NAEP data, math gap narrowing from 46 points in 1973 to 27 in 2004) reflects decades of many simultaneous social changes, not just stereotype-threat interventions.

### Intelligence is trainable and malleable — but the size and durability of gains is contested
- **Design**: (a) Jaeggi et al. (PNAS, 2008) — 34 trained vs. 35 control subjects; daily 25-minute adaptive dual n-back training for 8–19 days; pre/post test on Raven's/BOMAT fluid-intelligence measures. (b) Blackwell et al. (*Child Development*, 2007) — tracked adolescents' implicit theory of intelligence ("entity" = fixed, vs. "growth/incremental" = can develop) against math grades across two years of middle school. (c) The Perry Preschool Study — 123 low-SES African-American children in Ypsilanti, MI (1962), randomly assigned to a high-quality active-learning preschool program (+ home visits) or no program, followed to age 40.
- **Result**: (a) trained group's IQ-test performance rose more than controls', with a dose-response relationship — more days of training, bigger post-test gain (~4.5-point gain at 19 days vs. near-zero at 8 days). (b) students holding an incremental/growth theory of intelligence gained on math grades across 7th–8th grade; students holding an entity/fixed theory showed flat-to-declining grades from the *same starting point*. (c) Perry-program children's IQ rose sharply by age 5 (96 vs. 80 at entry) but the two groups' IQ trajectories **converged by around age 10** (~85–88 in both groups) — yet at age 40 the program group showed dramatically better outcomes: fewer arrested 5+ times (36% vs. 55%), more earning $20K+ (60% vs. 40%), higher high-school graduation (65% vs. 45%), higher basic achievement at 14 (49% vs. 15%), more homework at 15 (61% vs. 38%).
- **Inference**: three independent strands converge on the same point — the IQ *score* is not the right unit to track if the question is "did the intervention work." Working-memory training moves the fluid-intelligence score short-term; growth mindset moves grades even without a stated IQ change; and Perry shows the most dramatic result of all — the IQ boost from preschool **faded entirely**, yet real-life outcomes 30 years later diverged enormously, meaning whatever the preschool program changed, it wasn't sustained IQ-test points.
- **Weakness**: the Jaeggi n-back-training result was influential but became one of psychology's contested effects — later replications often showed transfer to the *specific* trained task without generalizing as broadly as first reported (not discussed on these slides, flagged in §9); Perry Preschool has a small sample (123) from one site and era, limiting generalizability; Blackwell is correlational on a self-reported belief, not an experimental manipulation of mindset.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| **g — single general factor** (Spearman's *g*; Carroll's three-stratum model puts *g* atop 8+ group factors from a meta-analysis of 450 studies; the lecture states "*g* remains best predictor in real life") | **Multiple Intelligences** (Gardner: linguistic, spatial, musical, logical-mathematical, bodily-kinesthetic, intra-/inter-personal, naturalist, existential — a *profile*, not one score) | The **positive manifold** (all cognitive subtests positively intercorrelate, replicated across hundreds of studies) is direct evidence for a dominant common factor; the slide itself flags Gardner's proposal with "? Measurement, skills" — no equivalent large-scale psychometric validation of nine independent, empirically separable intelligences is shown in this lecture, so the deck does not treat the two as evidentially symmetric even though it presents both |
| **Entity/fixed theory of intelligence** (ability comes from genes/the past, can't change much) | **Growth/incremental theory** (ability grows with effort and practice; Dweck) | Blackwell et al. (2007): adolescents starting at the *same* math grade level diverge over two years purely based on stated belief — growth-believers gain, entity-believers don't — a within-population comparison that isolates belief from starting ability |
| High heritability (~.50, twin/adoption data) means genes set the outcome | Heritability is compatible with large environmental effects (Flynn effect; breastfeeding +5–7 IQ points; birth weight; Hart & Risley language exposure; Perry Preschool) | The tomato-plant/Japan-height logic: heritability is a variance-partition *within the environments sampled*, not a ceiling — the Flynn effect (100→108→120 across three generations on the *same* test) is a 20-point shift with no time for genetic change, which only environment can produce |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: fronto-parietal network activated across high-*g* spatial and verbal contrasts (Duncan et al., 2000); dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and Broca's area scale activation with working-memory load (n-back); lower-IQ individuals show *more* prefrontal activation for equivalent performance (neural inefficiency); COMT genotype (dopamine metabolism) predicts WCST perseverative errors and n-back activation efficiency.
- Cognitive/information-processing: fluid intelligence (gF, novel-problem reasoning) vs. crystallized intelligence (known information); working memory capacity; mental speed (simple reaction time correlates with IQ); abstract/taxonomic vs. functional categorization strategies.
- Behavioral: WAIS/WISC verbal and performance subtest profiles; Raven's Progressive Matrices performance; math-grade trajectories by mindset; test performance swings under stereotype-threat framing.
- Social/cultural: Kpelle tribal categorization norms vs. Western schooled norms; US history of test-based racism (Immigration Act of 1924, "biologically weak stocks" rhetoric aimed at Italians and Jews); median household income by race as a background SES confound; NAEP achievement-gap trends over three decades; self-affirmation and expressive-writing classroom interventions.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- **Heritability ≠ fixity, and heritability says nothing about between-group causation.** This is the single most important boundary in the lecture and the one most often violated in public discussion: a trait can be highly heritable *within* a population/environment and still shift massively when the environment changes (Japanese-American height; the Flynn effect); by the same logic, a heritability estimate computed within Black or within White Americans separately cannot, by itself, tell you whether the *between*-group mean gap is genetic, environmental, or (per the German-GI-children data on this deck) apparently not genetic at all in that instance.
- **IQ score gains can fade while their downstream effects don't (Perry Preschool).** Program-group IQ converged with the no-program group by ~age 10, yet life outcomes at 40 were substantially different — so IQ-test score is not a reliable single indicator of whether an early intervention "worked."
- **Stereotype threat shows the *test situation itself* is not neutral.** A framing change or even listing race beforehand can create or erase a performance gap on identical items — so raw group-average score differences on a given test administration cannot be assumed to reflect a stable underlying capacity difference without ruling out situational confounds.
- **Re-norming changes who counts as "impaired."** The slide notes that when WISC-III was renormed in 1991, the number of people scoring below the mental-retardation cutoff (IQ 70) doubled — a purely psychometric artifact of renorming against the Flynn-effect-shifted population, not a real change in anyone's cognition.
- **Gardner's Multiple Intelligences lacks the same evidentiary base as *g*.** The lecture itself marks this with "? Measurement, skills" — an acknowledged gap, not a settled finding.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that IQ is "real" but not simply "fixed by genes," I would cite the Flynn effect: the same test given to genetically similar populations three generations apart shows a ~20-point rise (100→108→120), which no genetic story explains over three generations, alongside the twin-study data showing genes do matter within a generation. Both facts have to be true together.
2. Rebuttal to "IQ gaps between groups just prove one group has better genes": cite the German postwar natural experiment — children of Black and White American GIs, raised by German mothers in the same environment, showed no IQ gap, even though the same comparison in the contemporaneous US does show a gap. If genes-by-race explained the US gap, removing the US environment shouldn't have removed the gap — but it did. Heritability computed within a group also cannot, by definition, speak to between-group causes.
3. Transfer: if a school reports that immigrant students score lower on a "test of general aptitude" administered in a foreign, timed, high-stakes format than on ordinary coursework, stereotype-threat/diagnostic-framing theory predicts that reframing the same test as "an ungraded practice exercise" (non-diagnostic) should narrow or eliminate the gap — testable directly, the way Steele's relabeling manipulation was.

## 9. Open Questions
- The slide reporting the Black–White IQ gap widening "0.6/year, 83.4" across ages 4–24 gives no citation or explanation of what "83.4" refers to — flagged here as unresolved rather than guessed at.
- The Herrnstein/Murray-style sibling table (IQ group vs. income vs. illegitimacy rate, slide 21) is presented with no author or study citation in the deck; its provenance and methodology are not verifiable from the slide alone.
- Working-memory training (Jaeggi et al., 2008) was highly influential but its claim that n-back training transfers broadly to fluid intelligence has been contested by later replication attempts not covered in this lecture — the deck presents the original positive result without that subsequent debate.
- This is, per the assignment brief, the most socially contested lecture in the course. The lecture's own position is explicit and worth stating plainly rather than editorializing further: heritability estimates describe within-population, within-environment variance; they are repeatedly and explicitly *not* extended in this lecture to claims about the causes of between-group differences, and the one piece of between-group evidence offered (the German GI children) points away from a genetic explanation for the US Black–White gap. Readers bringing outside claims about group differences to this material should notice that the lecture does not make those claims and takes some care (tomato analogy, Japan/height, German GI study) to explain why the statistic cannot support them.
- How much of the "*g* is the best real-world predictor" claim (slide 31) survives once stereotype threat, self-affirmation, and mindset effects are accounted for as measurement confounds on the predictor itself? Not addressed in this lecture.
