# Lec 19 — Stress

> One sentence: stress is not a property of the event but of the **response** to it — the same objective stressor produces wildly different physiological damage depending on whether it is appraised as predictable, controllable, improving, and shared, which is why "how stressed you are" cannot be read off a life-events checklist.

## 1. Driving Question
- Core question: what is stress, physiologically and psychologically, and why does an identical stimulus (a shock, a deadline, an insult) sometimes wreck the body and sometimes barely register?
- Why it isn't obvious: the folk model treats stress as an objective quantity attached to the event itself — a checklist of "stressful life events" (divorce, job loss, exams) that should predict illness on its own. That model predicts that two people facing the same stressor should show the same physiological cost.
- Gap from the previous lecture: [[Lec15-Emotion-and-Motivation]] established that an emotional response depends on the **cognitive appraisal** of a stimulus, not the stimulus alone (Schachter-Singer two-factor theory; the same arousal read as "excitement" or "fear" depending on context). This lecture runs the identical argument through the body's stress machinery: the rat given a warning bell before a shock, or a lever (even a disconnected one), or a companion to bite, shows a measurably smaller physiological stress response than a rat receiving the exact same shock alone. The stressor is constant; the appraisal of predictability, control, and support is what varies — and that is what the body tracks.

## 2. Key Concepts (max 5)
| Concept | One-line definition, in your own words | Easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| Stress（应激/压力反应） | The psychological and physiological response to a stimulus (stressor) that displaces the body from equilibrium — a response, not a property of the stimulus | Not the stressor itself; "stress" in this lecture always names the organism's reaction |
| General Adaptation Syndrome（一般适应综合征，GAS） | Selye's three-stage nonspecific response to any sustained stressor: alarm → resistance → exhaustion | Not disease-specific — the same three stages run whether the stressor is a toxin, a rat drop, or a deadline |
| HPA axis（下丘脑-垂体-肾上腺轴） | Hypothalamus → CRH → anterior pituitary → ACTH → adrenal cortex → glucocorticoids (cortisol); the hormonal arm of the stress response, slower than the sympathetic nervous system | Not the sympathetic "fight/flight" arm (epinephrine/norepinephrine, seconds); HPA runs on a 15-sec-to-minutes timescale |
| Cognitive appraisal of a stressor（认知评价） | Predictability, perceived control, trajectory (improving vs. worsening), and social support each independently shift the physiological cost of an *identical* objective stressor | Not "positive thinking" — the effect shows up in rats and shows up even when control is illusory (a disconnected lever still helps) |
| Embodied cognition / shared pain pathway（具身认知） | Social pain (rejection, exclusion) recruits some of the same neural machinery (dACC) as physical pain's affective component, not just a linguistic metaphor | Not "social pain literally feels physical" — the overlap is in the *affective/suffering* component of pain, not the sensory-localization component |

## 3. Evidence Chain

### Selye's discovery of the General Adaptation Syndrome (1930s)
- **Design**: Selye, tasked with finding the function of an ovarian extract a colleague had isolated, injected rats daily and later autopsied them (Selye described himself as "not so good at handling rats" — he dropped them, chased them). Crucially, he ran **control** rats injected with saline/other substances and physically handled the same way.
- **Result**: months later, both extract-injected and control rats showed the same triad — peptic ulcers, enlarged adrenal glands, shrunken immune (lymphoid) tissue. Exposing rats to many *different* stressors produced the *same* triad.
- **Inference**: because the specific chemical was irrelevant and only the nonspecific handling/stress mattered, Selye reframed his own failed experiment as evidence for a **general, stressor-independent physiological response** — the General Adaptation Syndrome — rather than a specific effect of the ovarian hormone. The three stages (per the session's Check-Yourself answer): **alarm** (sympathetic activation, cortisol release, primary appraisal of the threat), **resistance** (resources mobilized, sustained cortisol if the stressor is chronic), **exhaustion** (resources depleted, damage accrues — this is where disease appears).
- **Weakness**: an accidental finding turned into a theory after the fact; no manipulation isolated *which* feature of "being handled/injected" was doing the work, so GAS names a pattern more than it explains a mechanism.

### The stress-response machinery and why chronic stress is pathological
- **Design/description** (mechanism, not a single study): two parallel systems. **Sympathetic nervous system** — brain → spine → organs/vessels/sweat glands/muscles/hair; supports the "four Fs" (flight, fright, fight, sex) via epinephrine/norepinephrine; opposed by the **parasympathetic** system (sleep, eating, relaxation). Layered on top, the slower **HPA axis**: hypothalamus releases CRH → anterior pituitary releases ACTH (~15 sec) → adrenal glands release glucocorticoids/cortisol (a few minutes).
- **Result**: the lecture's central table pairs each **adaptive, short-term** stress response with the **disorder** that appears if the same response runs chronically — mobilization of energy → myopathy, fatigue, diabetes; increased cardiovascular tone → stress-induced hypertension (ventricular hypertrophy is described as "the top predictor of cardiac arrest, controlling for age"; damage to arteries via plaque and inflamed vessels); suppression of digestion → ulceration, colitis; suppression of growth → psychogenic dwarfism; suppression of reproduction → amenorrhea, impotency, loss of libido; suppression of immune system → increased disease risk; sharpening of cognition → neuron death.
- **Inference**: this is the lecture's master argument for *why* stress matters medically — every piece of the acute stress response is adaptive (it reallocates resources toward an emergency), and every piece becomes a specific disease if the "emergency" never ends. It also explains the **mortality shift**: 1900's leading killers were acute infectious causes (pneumonia, tuberculosis, the 1918 influenza — "more than WWI" — and childbirth); by 2007 the leading killers were heart disease, cancer, and cerebrovascular disease, i.e., diseases of **cumulative, chronic** physiological wear — exactly what the adaptive/disorder table predicts once acute infectious threats are mostly controlled.
- **Weakness / evidentiary caution (two specific caveats the lecture flags)**: (i) peptic ulcers, the textbook "stress disease," are substantially caused by *Helicobacter pylori* infection (Warren & Marshall, 2005 Nobel Prize; antibiotic treatment cures many cases) — yet only ~10% of people infected with H. pylori develop ulcers, and ~15% of ulcer cases are unrelated to the bacterium, so stress is at most a **co-factor/moderator**, not the sole or even primary cause — a caution against over-attributing any single disease to "stress" alone. (ii) **Type A personality** (Friedman & Rosenman, 1960s: competitive, over-achieving, time-pressured, impatient, hostile → cardiovascular risk comparable to smoking or high cholesterol) largely **failed to replicate as a global construct**; what survived repeated replication is specifically the **hostility** component (mistrust, expectation of harm/provocation, cynicism, suppressed hostile expression) — a case study in a broad personality-disease claim being narrowed down to its one replicable "active ingredient."

### Anticipation itself is a stressor: dread (Berns et al., 2006, *Science*)
- **Design**: fMRI while participants awaited a cutaneous electric shock to the foot. Each trial specified a voltage level and a wait time; in a choice phase participants could choose, e.g., 90% voltage delivered in 3 seconds vs. 60% voltage delivered in 27 seconds.
- **Result**: some participants ("extreme dreaders") preferred **more pain sooner** over less pain after a longer wait; brain activation (including somatosensory and cingulate regions) scaled with voltage, and extreme dreaders showed earlier, more sustained activation between the warning cue and the shock itself than mild dreaders.
- **Inference**: the objective stimulus (shock intensity) is held constant across the comparison of interest here — what varies is the anticipatory period, and it is costly enough that some people will pay in physical pain to shorten it. This is the physiological-response demonstration that the *waiting/appraisal* period, not just the stimulus, carries a stress cost — setting up the psychological-modifiers evidence below.
- **Weakness**: small clinical/lab paradigm (foot shock), individual-differences framing ("dreaders") is descriptive, not yet mechanistic.

### Psychological modifiers of the stress response — the same objective stressor, manipulated appraisal
- **Outlets for frustration (Jay Weiss)**: rats given only a mild shock show a prolonged stress response (heart rate, glucocorticoid secretion, ulcers); rats able to gnaw a wooden bar, eat, drink, run a wheel, or **bite another rat** show fewer ulcers under the identical shock. (Real-world echo: baboons attack bystanders after losing a status fight.)
- **Predictability**: a warning bell before a shock reduces ulcers relative to an unsignaled shock; intermittent/random food delivery raises glucocorticoids relative to scheduled delivery of the *same total food*.
- **Control, including illusory control**: a rat given a lever shows a reduced stress response even when the **lever is disconnected** from the shock; in a human noxious-noise paradigm, the person with a stop button is less hypertensive **whether or not the button is ever pressed**; occupational-stress research finds high-demand/low-control jobs the most damaging combination.
- **Rodin & Langer (1977) — nursing home, perceived control and mortality**: **Design**: residents randomly assigned to Group A (choices about visitors, movie nights, which houseplant to keep) vs. Group B (no choices; given a plant but staff tended it). **Result**: 1.5 years later, Group A was more cheerful, active, alert, and healthier, and **half as many had died**. **Inference**: the objective environment (a plant, a schedule) was nearly identical; only the appraisal of agency differed, and it predicted mortality — among the strongest field demonstrations that appraisal, not stimulus, drives physiological cost. **Weakness**: single site, no biological mediator (cortisol, etc.) measured; mortality difference could partly reflect a third factor correlated with willingness/ability to exercise choice.
- **Culture moderates whether "having a choice" helps**: elementary-school children (Anglo-American vs. Asian-American) worked hardest on an anagram task under three random conditions (self-chose the task category, teacher chose, mother chose). Anglo-Americans persisted ~4× longer when *they* chose vs. teacher-chosen; Asian-Americans persisted **~30% longer when mother chose** than when they chose themselves, and 2× longer than teacher-chosen. **Inference**: this directly falsifies a naive "self-determined control always reduces stress/raises motivation" rule — the *same* objective condition ("someone else decided") is appraised as controlling in an individualist frame and as supportive/trust-affirming in a collectivist frame. This is the cleanest evidence in the lecture that it is the **appraisal**, culturally shaped, doing the causal work.
- **Richter (1957) — learned hope**: rats placed in a jar of water show huge variance in swim time before giving up (15 minutes to 60 hours); rats that were first picked up, allowed to struggle free, and returned to the water average **60 hours** of continued effort. **Inference**: a single prior experience of successful escape changes the appraisal of the *same* inescapable-seeming situation from hopeless to worth continued effort. **Weakness**: dated and ethically fraught methodology; small, likely selectively reported effect sizes.
- **Perception of trajectory (improving/worsening)**: two rats receive different shock rates (10/hr vs. 50/hr); on day 2 both are switched to 25/hr — the rat whose rate *increased* (10→25) shows less hypertension than the rat whose rate *decreased* (50→25), even though both now receive the identical objective stimulus. **Inference**: the body tracks the **direction of change relative to a reference point**, not the absolute level — a stress analogue of relative/reference-dependent judgment.
- **Social support**: primates show a worse glucocorticoid stress response among strangers than among friends after a stressor; humans show reduced cardiovascular reactivity to public speaking, math tasks, or arguments when a supportive friend is present; epidemiologically, people with spouses/close friends live longer, mortality risk rises after a spouse's death, bereaved parents of children killed in war show elevated disease/mortality risk **only if** divorced or widowed (i.e., lacking a partner), and coronary patients lacking social support have **3× the death rate over 5 years**.
- **Composite ranking (the lecture's own summary)**: ordered from most to least ulcer-inducing for what is nominally "the same shock" — shock alone > shock + another rat to bite > shock + gnawing bar > shock + warning signal > shock + lever > shock reduced from 50→25/hr > shock + friends present. **Inference (overall)**: this ranking *is* the lecture's cognitive-appraisal spine made concrete — outlet, predictability, control, favorable trajectory, and social support are five independent levers that each downgrade the physiological cost of one constant objective stressor.

### PTSD and the hippocampus: consequence or pre-existing vulnerability?
- **Background**: PTSD is a severe anxiety disorder that can develop after exposure to psychologically traumatic events (assault, rape, combat), with re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmares), increased arousal, and hypervigilance; it develops in roughly **20%** of those exposed to a qualifying trauma. PTSD patients show smaller hippocampal volumes on average (with variability across studies) — but smaller hippocampus could be scar tissue from the trauma (consequence) or a pre-existing risk factor (diathesis) that made trauma harder to withstand.
- **Design (Gilbertson et al., 2002, *Nature Neuroscience*)**: monozygotic twin pairs discordant for combat exposure (one twin deployed to combat, the co-twin not), comparing hippocampal volume and PTSD severity.
- **Result**: greater PTSD severity in the combat-exposed twin was associated with **smaller hippocampal volume in both twins** — including the twin who was never in combat.
- **Inference**: if a small hippocampus were purely a consequence of trauma, only the exposed twin's volume should track symptom severity. The fact that the **unexposed co-twin's** hippocampal volume also predicts the exposed twin's PTSD severity rules out a pure-consequence account and supports smaller hippocampal volume as a **pre-existing vulnerability marker** (shared genetically/developmentally between twins) rather than solely trauma-induced damage.
- **Complementary design (Admon et al., 2009, *PNAS*)**: prospective study of 50 Israeli military recruits, scanned before and after stressful military service. **Result**: amygdala reactivity **measured before** stress exposure predicted the *increase* in stress symptoms afterward; hippocampal change over time correlated with symptoms in a content-specific way.
- **Inference**: this adds a genuinely prospective, predictive (not just retrospective/correlational) layer — baseline amygdala reactivity is a candidate causal antecedent of later symptom severity, converging with the twin study's vulnerability-marker conclusion.
- **Weakness**: twin design is elegant but a small, specialized sample (combat-exposed twin pairs are rare); the prospective study (n=50) is modest and correlational for the hippocampal-change finding even though predictive for amygdala reactivity.

### Embodied cognition: does social pain borrow the physical pain system?
- **Background/concept**: embodied cognition proposes that abstract thought (including emotional concepts) is shaped by bodily experience. Pain itself fractionates into a **sensory** component (objective: location, intensity — somatosensory cortex) and an **affective** component (subjective: unpleasantness, suffering — cingulate cortex).
- **Rainville et al. (1997, *Science*)**: **Design**: hypnotic suggestion of high or low pain *affect* while the physical (nociceptive) stimulus was held constant. **Result**: anterior cingulate activity tracked the hypnotically suggested affect; somatosensory cortex did not. **Inference**: this dissociates pain's "how bad does it feel" component from its "where/how intense" component at the neural level — a double dissociation within a single experience, and it is the finding the rest of the chain builds on (affect lives in ACC, not S1).
- **Eisenberger & Lieberman (2004, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, reporting the Cyberball paradigm)**: **Design**: fMRI during a virtual ball-tossing game where participants were included in one round and excluded in another. **Result**: social exclusion increased dorsal ACC (dACC) activity, and self-reported distress correlated with dACC activity during exclusion. **Inference**: the same region carrying physical pain's affective component responds to purely social exclusion, suggesting a shared "alarm" system rather than two independent systems that merely share vocabulary.
- **Kross et al. (2011, *PNAS*)** extended this to real heartbreak: recently rejected participants viewed ex-partner photos while recalling the breakup (vs. viewing friends' photos/positive memories) alongside physical heat pain, and found neural overlap between romantic-rejection activation and physical-pain activation (including insula/ACC regions).
- **Causal test — DeWall et al. (acetaminophen RCT)**: **Design**: random assignment to 2000 mg/day acetaminophen (Tylenol/Excedrin) vs. placebo for 3 weeks, with daily self-reports. **Result**: by day 15, the acetaminophen group reported **less painful** subjective response to social rejection and showed **less brain response** to social rejection than placebo. **Inference**: this is the block that upgrades "shared neural correlates" to something closer to **shared causal machinery** — a drug with no known psychological/social mechanism of action, only an analgesic one, reduced a social-emotional outcome through random assignment. CorrelYaional overlap (Rainville, Eisenberger, Kross) cannot rule out that two separate systems just happen to co-activate; a causal manipulation of the physical-pain system that moves the social-pain outcome is much harder to explain that way.
- **Weakness (whole chain)**: overlapping activation (Eisenberger, Kross) is correlational and region-level, not proof of a shared computation; the acetaminophen effect, while causal, is a single mechanism (COX-mediated) and a modest effect size over a specific self-report window — it shows *some* shared machinery, not that social and physical pain are identical.

### Stress from social identity: stereotype threat
- **Concept (Steele, 1997)**: stereotype threat is the threat that others' judgments, or one's own actions, will confirm a negative stereotype about one's group; the resulting stress/vigilance can itself undermine performance on the very task the stereotype targets.
- **IAT demo**: in the classic Implicit Association Test pairing, most test-takers respond faster pairing "unpleasant" with "Black" than "unpleasant" with "White"; on the reverse pairing (pleasant + White vs. pleasant + Black), the fast/congruent pairing was shown by an estimated **75% of White and 50% of Black** test-takers, raising the question of whether this reflects automatic *associations* (culturally learned) or endorsed *attitudes* — the lecture explicitly separates these.
- **Richeson & Shelton (2003, *Psychological Science*), "When Prejudice Does Not Pay"**: **Design**: White participants completed the IAT, then had a videotaped conversation about the fraternity system and post-9/11 racial profiling with either a Black or a White experimenter, then performed a Stroop task, with interference measured as incompatible-trial minus control-trial reaction time. **Result**: not given quantitatively in the slide deck itself (design only). **Inference (as licensed by the design)**: pairing IAT bias scores with post-interaction executive-function cost tests whether *regulating* an implicit bias during an interracial interaction consumes cognitive resources — i.e., whether stereotype-related contact is a genuine, measurable stressor on cognitive control, not just a self-report phenomenon.
- **Weakness**: the deck does not display Richeson & Shelton's actual interference numbers, so the result direction/magnitude should not be asserted beyond "design tested whether interracial contact taxes executive function" — treat the specific finding as outside what this lecture's slides establish.

## 4. Competing Theories
| Position A | Position B | Which evidence discriminates them |
|---|---|---|
| Stress is a property of the objective stimulus/event (checklist/life-events model — bigger event = bigger stress response) | Stress is a property of the **cognitive appraisal** of the event (predictability, control, trajectory, support) | Weiss's rats and the Rodin & Langer nursing-home study: identical objective stressor/environment, different appraisal (outlet, choice), different physiological/mortality outcome |
| "Someone else decides for me" is inherently more stressful/demotivating (individualist assumption baked into most control research) | The *meaning* of who decides is culturally constructed — other-chosen can be more motivating than self-chosen | The Anglo-American vs. Asian-American anagram-persistence study: identical "mother chose" condition, opposite effect on effort by cultural group |
| Stress directly causes specific diseases (e.g., "stress causes ulcers") | Stress is a co-factor/moderator; a specific biological cause is often necessary (e.g., *H. pylori* for most peptic ulcers) | Only ~10% of *H. pylori*-infected people develop ulcers and ~15% of ulcers are *H. pylori*-negative — stress alone is neither necessary nor sufficient |
| Smaller hippocampal volume in PTSD is scar tissue caused by trauma | Smaller hippocampal volume is a pre-existing, heritable vulnerability marker | Gilbertson et al.'s discordant-twin design: the *unexposed* co-twin's hippocampal volume also predicts the exposed twin's PTSD severity |
| Social pain is a linguistic metaphor borrowing physical-pain vocabulary, with no shared underlying machinery | Social and physical pain share real neural/pharmacological machinery (at least the affective component) | DeWall's acetaminophen RCT: a physical analgesic, randomly assigned, causally reduces self-reported and neural response to social rejection |

## 5. Levels of Analysis
- Neural/physiological: sympathetic nervous system (epinephrine/norepinephrine, "four Fs") vs. parasympathetic; **HPA axis** — hypothalamus → CRH → anterior pituitary (~15 sec) → ACTH → adrenal cortex (minutes) → glucocorticoids/cortisol; amygdala and hippocampus in PTSD; somatosensory cortex (pain's sensory component) vs. anterior cingulate/insula (pain's affective component, shared with social pain, i.e., dACC in exclusion/rejection).
- Cognitive/information-processing: primary appraisal (is this dangerous?) and secondary appraisal (what can I do about it?) inherited from [[Lec15-Emotion-and-Motivation]]'s appraisal framework; perceived control and predictability as computations over the *same* sensory input; relative/reference-dependent coding of stressor trajectory (10→25 vs. 50→25); implicit associations (IAT) as automatic, possibly non-endorsed, cognitive structures.
- Behavioral: outlet behaviors (gnawing, running, biting — problem-focused analogues); coping strategies from the session's Check-Yourself material — **problem-focused coping** (active coping, planning, instrumental social support — changes the external stressor) vs. **emotion-focused coping** (emotional social support, venting, mental disengagement — changes the internal response); hypervigilance and flashbacks in PTSD; Stroop interference as a behavioral index of executive load after stereotype-relevant contact.
- Social/cultural: individualist vs. collectivist framing of who should choose (the Anglo-American/Asian-American anagram study); marriage/friendship as a mortality-relevant variable; stereotype threat is constitutively social — the stressor is the anticipated judgment of others, not a physical event at all.

## 6. Boundaries & Failure Cases
- **GAS is a description of a pattern, not a mechanism.** Selye's finding that "many different stressors produce the same triad" doesn't by itself explain *why* — it was, by his own account, an interpretation imposed after a failed, specific experiment.
- **"Stress causes disease X" claims need a base-rate check.** The ulcer story is the cautionary case: a plausible, widely believed stress-disease link turned out to require a bacterium in the large majority of cases; stress functions as a moderator of risk, not a sufficient cause.
- **Broad personality-disease constructs often don't survive replication.** Type A personality as a whole failed to replicate; only the narrower hostility component held up across studies — a caution against accepting a broad clinical construct because one famous early study supported it.
- **Perceived control is not uniformly stress-reducing.** The session's discussion material notes that being *responsible* for others' outcomes (e.g., leading a group project) can be more stressful than not having control at all — so "control reduces stress" needs the boundary condition that the control is wanted/manageable, not imposed as unwanted responsibility.
- **Psychogenic dwarfism (the Barrie/Peter Pan anecdote) is a single historical case**, not a controlled study — evocative but not evidence on its own for growth suppression via stress.
- **PTSD imaging findings are correlational/predictive, not fully causal**, and samples are small and specialized (discordant combat twins; 50 recruits) — real progress over pure correlation, but short of an experimental manipulation of hippocampal volume.
- **Neural co-activation (fMRI overlap) alone cannot establish shared mechanism** — this is exactly why the acetaminophen study matters; without it, the physical/social pain overlap claim would rest entirely on correlational imaging.

- One concrete real-life example (my own): two people get the identical piece of critical feedback from a manager by email. One has a scheduled 1:1 the next day to discuss it (predictable, some control over the response) and hears from a colleague that the manager gives everyone tough feedback (favorable reattribution/social support); the other gets the email at 11pm with no scheduled follow-up and no one to check the story with. Same "stressor" (the email's content), very different GAS-style trajectory for each person — this is the Weiss/Rodin-Langer logic applied outside the lab.

## 8. Self-Test (close the notes first)
1. To prove to a non-psychologist that stress depends on appraisal rather than the stimulus, I'd cite Rodin & Langer's nursing home study: two groups got essentially the same objective environment, but the group given choices was healthier and had half the mortality 1.5 years later. A checklist model of stress ("nursing home = stressful event") predicts no difference between groups; only an appraisal model predicts this.
2. Rebuttal to "of course stress causes disease, that's obvious": the ulcer case complicates "obvious" — peptic ulcers are mostly caused by *H. pylori*, discovered by researchers who won a Nobel for overturning the stress-only folk model; only 10% of infected people get ulcers and 15% of ulcers occur without the bacterium, so stress is a moderating risk factor sitting on top of a specific biological cause, not a free-standing explanation.
3. Transfer: if a company introduces a "flexible hours" policy but employees report *more* stress, the appraisal framework predicts this happens when the added choice comes with added responsibility/uncertainty (the "group leader" boundary case) or clashes with a cultural norm about who should set schedules (the individualist/collectivist finding) — not that "more control" mechanically failed, but that the manipulation changed appraisal in an unintended direction.
